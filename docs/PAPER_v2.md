# Shape bias is a readout property: a developmental two-stream model, frozen networks and human cortex

## Abstract

Humans name objects by shape when shape and texture disagree, yet the ventral stream is well described by texture statistics. We test this with a two-stream network modelling magnocellular and parvocellular input: one ResNet-18 receives a cone-opponent image's luminance under a developmental diet, the other its L−M channel. The magno stream's shape bias starts in its input and survives training only under the diet and beside the second stream; the parvo stream's texture bias is learnt. Fitted to eight Natural Scenes Dataset subjects, the texture-biased stream carries every extrastriate region. Behaviour, reading out from both, is shape-biased; one readout weight moves the rate from 0.40 to 0.90, and the weight best predicting extrastriate cortex (0.33) lies below the one best predicting V1 (0.58). In 38 frozen ImageNet networks without a stream split, a reciprocal gain on each network's shape- and texture-predictive subspaces moves the rate by 0.08–0.29 on unseen exemplars within five points of top-1, re-ordering 19% of pairs, twice the resampling noise; it acts mostly on texture evidence, except in a Stylized-ImageNet network with a shape-leaning representation; and agreement with observers follows the rate. Shape-biased behaviour does not require shape-biased representations; the cue-conflict rate is a readout statistic.

## 1. Introduction

Humans presented with an image whose shape and texture point to different categories name the shape: on the cue-conflict images of Geirhos et al. (2018a), ten observers did so on 96% of decided trials, while ImageNet-trained convolutional networks named the texture. Closing that gap is a standard target for biologically motivated models (Hermann et al., 2020; Jang & Tong, 2021), and a developmental visual diet — input whose acuity, contrast and chromatic sensitivity follow the human developmental trajectory — is among the most effective routes to it (Lu et al., 2026).

A second body of evidence sits uneasily beside the first: ventral-stream representations are well described by texture statistics — category-selective human cortex fails to discriminate natural images from texture-matched scrambles yet still discriminates categories (Jagadeesh & Gardner, 2022), macaque V4 responses to natural textures are predicted by their image statistics (Okazawa et al., 2015), and in inferotemporal cortex texture is the more decodable cue (Jagadeesh & Livingstone, 2024; Komatsu & Goda, 2018). The argument needs only that the ventral description is substantially texture-like, not that shape is absent (the evidence is one human study and several macaque ones).

If behaviour is shape-biased and the ventral stream texture-biased, either the two measures disagree about what shape bias is, or behaviour is not a readout of one representation. The second horn is a hypothesis about architecture that single-trunk models cannot express, and two facts make it concrete: the visual system receives a luminance-dominated, coarse magnocellular signal and a chromatically opponent, finer parvocellular one, separated before cortex and later recombined (Livingstone & Hubel, 1987; Kaplan & Shapley, 1982; Derrington et al., 1984; Nassi & Callaway, 2009; Ferrera et al., 1994), and that input is not stationary over development, so the system first learns from a low-passed world. The two-stream hypothesis (Mishkin et al., 1983; Goodale & Milner, 1992) and the emergence of ventral- and dorsal-like streams in a two-pathway network trained on one objective (Bakhtiari et al., 2021) motivate an architecture whose streams are defined by their subcortical-like inputs rather than by their cortical targets.

This paper asks two questions, in the order the originating study posed them (Manocchio, 2026): can two streams fed magnocellular- and parvocellular-like inputs diverge while optimising a single objective and producing shape-biased behaviour, and does such a system explain how the ventral stream can be texture-like while behaviour is shape-biased? We build it — two ResNet-18 trunks fed the luminance and L−M channels of a cone-opponent image, with a developmental diet on the luminance channel (Fig. 1) — and examine its streams with probes and cue-conflict behaviour (Fig. 2), encoding models of eight subjects' 7T fMRI responses (Fig. 3) and the readout that combines them (Fig. 4). The streams diverge by different routes; the texture-biased one carries extrastriate cortex; and the integrated behaviour is shape-biased because a behavioural rate is a property of a readout over components, not a report of any one of them — a property that holds, in kind, in networks with no stream split at all (Fig. 6). Robustness, agreement with human decisions and representational dimensionality all come apart from the rate, as the account predicts (Fig. 5).

## 2. Results

### 2.1 The model, and what the diet costs

The network splits its input once, before any convolution (Fig. 1a): each image is converted to the DKL cone-opponent space, stream 1 receives the luminance channel and stream 2 the L−M channel, and each is a ResNet-18 trunk. Under the developmental diet the luminance channel passes through a Gaussian blur (σ = 4 px) and the contrast-sensitivity cut of Lu et al. (2026) at a fixed age of 48 months (Methods 4.1). The trunks are initialised bit-identically, so every stream difference below is attributable to the inputs, and a linear classifier reads out w·y₁ + (1−w)·y₂ of the two streams' penultimate features, with w = 0.5 during training.

Four front-end conditions are used throughout (Fig. 1a): the diet; the no-diet control; a channel-swap control applying the identical transform to the L−M channel instead; and a `parvo_highsf` control in which stream 2 additionally receives untransformed luminance, because real parvocellular cells carry both. Networks were trained on MiniEcoset (112 classes; Thorat & Kietzmann, 2024) for 150 epochs, three seeds per condition (Methods 4.2).

The diet costs accuracy (Fig. 1c): it reaches 0.556 validation top-1 at its best saved checkpoint against 0.701 without it and, uniquely, degrades after its peak (final 0.445), so behavioural and out-of-distribution results use each seed's best saved checkpoint, stated wherever the policy matters (Methods 4.7). Two single-stream controls trained with the identical diet — a ResNet-18 (11.2M parameters) and a ResNet-34 parameter-matched to the two-stream model (21.3M against 22.4M) — reach 0.599 and 0.623, so the accuracy cost of the split is not a capacity artefact (Fig. 4c).

### 2.2 The two streams specialise in opposite directions, by different routes

Read out separately with a linear probe on the cue-conflict images, the diet-trained network's two streams end on opposite sides of the shape–texture boundary: on exemplars the probe was not trained on, shape − texture accuracy is +0.337 for the magno stream and −0.168 for the parvo stream, means of three seeds, every seed on its side (Fig. 2a; +0.819 and −0.518 with exemplars shared; Methods 4.3); the magno stream decodes a new exemplar's texture at chance (0.08, against 0.0625). Behaviour agrees: read out alone, the magno stream's cue-conflict shape bias is 0.878 and the parvo stream's 0.418, against 0.675 for the integrated readout (Table 1), the divergence the thesis reported on one model per condition, now replicated across seeds.

The decomposition arises asymmetrically (Fig. 2a, Table 1). On the untrained network, most of the magno stream's shape bias is already present (+0.210 of the trained +0.337 on held-out exemplars), put there by the transform; the parvo stream begins nearly unbiased (−0.033) and acquires its texture preference over training. The shape-biased component starts in the input, the texture-biased component is built by learning, and what ordinary training does to the input's contribution is the decisive contrast: with the transform removed, the untrained luminance stream starts lower (+0.090) and training drives it across the sign flip to −0.122 — while the parvo stream ends texture-leaning whether or not the other stream is dieted (−0.168 against −0.118). Nor does the dieted input keep its bias alone: a single ResNet-18 trained on the magno stream's exact input and recipe ends at a rate of 0.557 and a penultimate-layer probe of −0.06 to −0.10 on held-out exemplars, where the same input as one of two streams ends at 0.878 and +0.26 to +0.27 — the shape-biased component survives training beside a second stream.

Which property of the input supplies the shape bias? Crossing the carrier (achromatic luminance or chromatic L−M) with the transform gives four cells, two of them from the swap and `parvo_highsf` conditions (Fig. 2b, c): on the held-out probe, the chromatic carrier gives −0.118 without the transform and −0.002 with it, the achromatic carrier −0.139 without and +0.337 with, so only the conjunction crosses zero, with an interaction of +0.36 (+0.73 with exemplars shared) and no seed overlap between the conjunction cell and any other; cue-conflict shape bias on the same four cells replicates it (Supplementary Table S3). The shape bias requires the developmental transform applied specifically to an achromatic channel — a relational fact about what a stream receives, not a property of a stream called “magno”.

Two qualifications belong here. First, cue-conflict shape bias is computed only over images a readout decides, a count that varies four-fold across cells (165 to 598 of 1,200; Fig. 2d); the probe, which classifies every image, and a forced two-way estimator over every image preserve every ordering this paper draws a conclusion from (Supplementary Table S9). Second, the diet-trained models are less accurate: we pre-registered the fatal outcome — a no-diet model at the diet's accuracy with a shape-biased magno stream — and retrained the no-diet network, selecting by rule in each of three seeds the epoch nearest the diet's accuracy; matching accuracy closes 4.3% of the probe gap on held-out exemplars (1.5–5.9% across seeds; 7.2% with exemplars shared) and 6.1% of the behavioural gap, and the matched magno stream stays on the texture side in every seed (−0.10 to −0.12; Fig. 2e, f; Supplementary Note S4).

### 2.3 In cortex, the texture-biased stream carries the ventral end of the hierarchy

The thesis's representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008) against seven NSD regions found that whichever stream was the more predictive correlated better with every region, a general factor that a correlation cannot separate from regional structure. We replaced it with voxel-wise banded-ridge encoding models for each of the eight NSD subjects, one band per stream, partitioning each voxel's cross-validated explained variance into the part only the magno band accounts for (U_m) and the part only the parvo band does (U_p) (Methods 4.5); Fig. 3a plots the per-voxel index (U_m − U_p)/(U_m + U_p), median over reliable voxels per ROI, across V1v → hV4.

The global stream preference differs between the arms — every retinotopic area is magno-explained without the diet and parvo-explained with it — and is not the result. The result is the shape of the profile: only the diet arm descends steeply from V1 to hV4, the direction a luminance/low-spatial-frequency versus chromatic split predicts, and it does so in the model whose global preference points the other way.

Quantified as the contrast hV4 − mean(V1v, V1d) of the per-ROI median (Fig. 3b; Extended Data Table 1), the gradient is −0.745 to −0.834 across the three diet-trained seeds against −0.049 to −0.096 without the diet, with the same separation on the raw statistic U_m − U_p, and all six comparisons reach the exact Wilcoxon floor for eight subjects (p = .0078) with complete seed separation. The per-subject Spearman ρ between hierarchical rank and stream preference is −0.964 to −0.973 with the diet and −0.46 to −0.66 without (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table S1); among the 630 labellings of the seven ROIs by hierarchical rank the diet arm's ρ is the single most extreme in every seed (p = .0016), a calibration of a result that was not pre-specified, and the no-diet arm's is not (Supplementary Note S4).

The diet does not create the gradient; it decides what training does to the one the input supplies. Untrained networks already sit at −0.544 on the diet front end and −0.479 on the other, so the front ends alone impose no different gradient; training with the diet steepens it and training without it erodes it to near zero (Fig. 3b). An independently trained checkpoint with the thesis's progressive developmental schedule gives −0.793 (bias-corrected displacements in Supplementary Note S4).

Pushed through models fitted on natural scenes only, the cue-conflict images give a predicted magno − parvo shape-bias difference of +1.53 to +1.57 with the diet against +0.08 to +0.11 without (8/8 subjects in every seed; Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table S2), a test that needs neither probe nor readout.

**The gradient survives its nuisance axes and the instrument's own bias.** The diet gradient holds within every eccentricity band and pRF-size tertile and on continuous pRF eccentricity and size, and the estimator's own simulated bias is small, flat and of the wrong sign (Extended Data Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables S7 and S8; Supplementary Note S3).

**The gradient does not stop at hV4, and it does not turn.** On NSD's full atlases (Extended Data Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S10) the diet index runs from −0.05 in V1v to −0.81 in hV4 and then sits between −0.81 and −0.94 in every extrastriate region — ventral, lateral and dorsal alike, in every atlas — with every region-minus-V1 contrast negative in 8/8 subjects (p = .0078) and no region-minus-region contrast departing from zero under either arm (all p > .05). Beyond V3 the diet's magno band contributes about 0.01 unique variance (Extended Data Fig. 4b): the texture-biased stream carries extrastriate cortex as a whole, and the thesis's magno→dorsal mapping fails for want of any magno variance to map.

Four things this section does not settle. First, the diet predicts BOLD worse, not better (noise-ceiling-normalised median r² 0.405 against 0.464, 8/8 subjects; Fig. 3e); its case rests on stream differentiation and the gradient. Second, the two front-end controls are degenerate on this instrument (Methods 4.5). Third, the gradient was not pre-specified: it is the survivor of seven regional hypotheses, six of which failed, and is read as sign and ordering rather than as a calibrated effect (Supplementary Notes S1 and S3). Fourth, “texture-biased” names the stream, not the reason cortex prefers it: without the diet the two streams lean to texture equally on held-out exemplars (−0.122 and −0.118; Results 2.2), and with it the stream carrying extrastriate cortex is both the texture-leaning one and the one whose input was not low-passed, so the design cannot separate texture bias from bandwidth.

### 2.4 Behaviour reads out from both streams, and the rate is a property of the readout

The integrated readout's cue-conflict shape bias is 0.675 ± 0.025 with the diet against 0.416 ± 0.020 without (mean ± s.d. over three seeds; Table 1), against 0.958 for the ten human observers and 0.172–0.429 for the CNNs of Geirhos et al. (2018a) on the identical images and statistic; integration costs 0.203 of the magno stream's 0.878 (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Note S4).

A readout of two oppositely biased components is nearly forced to land between them; what can be measured is how much of the rate it controls. Sweeping w while holding y₁ and y₂ exactly fixed (Fig. 4a) runs cue-conflict shape bias from 0.40 to 0.90 over 21 values of w, three seeds and both checkpoint policies, and w = 0.5 reproduces each trained model exactly. Because that sweep interpolates between two measured endpoints, we refitted a two-layer head on the frozen features at each w under a rule fixed in advance (Fig. 4b): its median span is 0.466 against 0.508 for the frozen affine head (92% of the pre-registered baseline, threshold 80%), and 0.283 when restricted to readouts within 5 points of peak validation accuracy. The span is a property of two oppositely biased components under any readout, and the extreme rates are bought with accuracy. Nothing here fits w to humans, and it could not: no observer's rate is reachable in any run (0 of 90 observer-runs), and a weight fitted to an observer's trial-by-trial choices does not cross-validate (Supplementary Note S4).

**The readout weight cortex prefers descends the hierarchy.** For each w, the readout's input z(w) = w·y₁ + (1 − w)·y₂ was the design of a ridge encoding model of every voxel in NSD's general visual mask, and the w that maximises an ROI's median r² is the weight that region prefers (Methods 4.5; Extended Data Fig. 5; Extended Data Table 2). Under the diet that weight falls from 0.58 in V1 to 0.33–0.35 in every extrastriate group, below V1 in 8/8 subjects (Δ = −0.252 [−0.283, −0.219], exact p = .0078); without the diet the gradient reverses (0.65 in V1 to 0.75–1.00 beyond hV4; 7/8). The weight that best reproduces the observers' choices is not identified by these data (median 0.67 over observers, but per-fit bootstrap intervals average 0.69 wide on [0, 1] and only 2 of 30 fits beat a permutation null), so we do not claim that cortex and behaviour prefer different weights. The curves are shallow: this is an ordering of optima, not an r² difference (Supplementary Note S4).

**Without a stream split, a gain on a network's own shape- and texture-predictive subspaces still moves the rate.** The span of Fig. 4a is given by construction in a two-stream network, so we tested networks with no split, under plans committed before each analysis ran (Methods 4.8). In a frozen network's penultimate features, the fifteen discriminant directions of a 16-class shape and of a 16-class texture linear discriminant, made mutually orthogonal, span a shape- and a texture-predictive subspace; a gain of 2w on the first and 2(1 − w) on the second, the network's own head untouched, is the analogue of the mixing weight, and every w is gated on top-1 accuracy on held-out natural images (Fig. 6). The 1,200 cue-conflict images reuse 160 object and 48 texture exemplars, so the subspaces, and the bases the gate is measured on, are fitted on images sharing no exemplar with those the rate is scored on; the pre-registered split shared exemplars, and its values are given as exemplar-shared (Methods 4.8). In the six ImageNet networks of Fig. 6 the rate rises monotonically with w by 0.14–0.20, a third of the two-stream span, so the pre-stated threshold (≥ 0.20 in at least four of the six), met in all six with exemplars shared, is met in one (Fig. 6a, b; Extended Data Tables 3 and 4).

Across the field's zoo the result holds and the ranking does not. In 38 torchvision ImageNet classifiers the gated span is 0.08–0.29 (median 0.18; 0.20–0.50 with exemplars shared) and unrelated to the trained rate (ρ = +0.17). Ranked by trained rate and by the rate at the shape end of the gate, the networks agree at Kendall τ = 0.63, with 19% of the 703 pairs reversing. Re-estimated on resampled data, the trained ranking itself reverses 8.9% of pairs (images resampled), 9.1% (object exemplars) and 13.6% [8.3%, 20.9%] (texture exemplars), so against the strictest floor the all-pairs figure is not decisive; among the pairs the trained rate resolves, 39 of 490 reverse under image resampling (8.0%, against a floor of 0.33%) and 12 of 389 under texture-exemplar resampling (3.1%, against 0.47%). A linear probe's shape-minus-texture accuracy ranks the zoo differently again (τ = 0.43; Extended Data Fig. 6; Extended Data Table 4). A gain on random 15-dimensional subspaces moves no network's rate by more than 0.08 (Supplementary Table S14).

The informative exception is a ResNet-50 trained on Stylized-ImageNet (Geirhos et al., 2018a): the highest trained rate of the 42 networks tested (0.81), the only one whose probe leans to shape on exemplars it was not trained on (+0.23 [+0.13, +0.34], against −0.52 to +0.01 for the ImageNet-trained networks), and the highest agreement with the observers (40% of the ceiling at its trained readout). Its bias sits in the representation, which the rate alone could not show, yet its span is not exceptional (0.11 on unseen exemplars, the 2nd smallest of 39); what sets it apart is which evidence the gain acts on. In a post hoc decomposition under a fifth plan (Methods 4.8), the gain moves 37 of the 38 ImageNet networks toward shape mainly by removing texture evidence (median 89% of the effect) and the Stylized-ImageNet network mainly by adding shape evidence (texture share 11%; Supplementary Table S14).

**Cortex loads on the texture-leaning units, not on the discriminant subspaces.** With the two unit halves of the no-diet single trunk as encoding bands, the texture-leaning half carries every extrastriate group relative to V1 in 8/8 subjects (Δ = −0.32, exact p = .0078), and the cortex-optimal weight of the lossy sum falls from 0.80 in V1 to 0.37–0.48 beyond hV4 (7/8, p = .023) while the behavioural rate rises toward w = 1 (Fig. 6c, d; Supplementary Table S12). In ImageNet networks the texture-leaning half carries early visual cortex as well, so the V1-relative prediction fails there, and the discriminant subspaces that move the rate are nearly silent as unique cortical predictors (at most 1.3% of normalised unique variance each), so the cortical claim is made under the unit rule only (Supplementary Note S4). The architecture is therefore not what makes the rate a readout property, nor, in the networks trained on our images, what makes extrastriate cortex load on the texture-biased component; it makes the components separable enough for one weight to move the rate across the range models occupy at no cost in accuracy, and for cortex to be carried almost entirely by one of them.

This is the mechanism we propose for the paradox: a system holding a shape-biased and a texture-biased component, whose behaviour reads out from both, will look shape-biased behaviourally and texture-biased wherever the texture-biased component dominates — and in this model of the subcortical split, the component that dominates the ventral end is the texture-biased one. It is an existence proof with a cortically grounded demonstration, not a measurement of the human readout.

### 2.5 Properties that do not follow from shape bias

If shape bias is a property of a readout over components, quantities the field treats as travelling with it need not, and over identical inputs three do not (Fig. 5).

**Robustness.** The standard reading of Geirhos et al. (2018a, 2021) is that shape bias buys out-of-distribution robustness. We pre-registered that prediction and tested it on corruptions applied before the front end (Methods 4.6; Fig. 5a; Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S5). The diet-trained model is less robust on four of the five corruption families it was not trained on (uniform noise, high-pass, false colour and rotation, with complete seed separation on each), equivalent on phase scrambling, and more robust only to low-pass, which we declared trained-in before the run because the diet is a low-pass blur. Robustness appears exactly where it was trained in and nowhere else, evidence that the nulls are real rather than a broken measurement. The coupling belongs to the stylisation route of Geirhos et al., not to shape bias; against the source of the diet we reproduce its shape-based half and not its robust half (Supplementary Note S4; Methods 4.6).

**Agreement with human decisions.** Error consistency — Cohen's κ on trial-by-trial agreement — subtracts the agreement two decision-makers' marginal rates force (Geirhos et al., 2020); across the ten observers the human–human ceiling is κ = 0.234. We pre-registered that the diet-trained model would be more human-consistent than its control; it is not, on either readout, and our most shape-biased model, the diet-trained magno stream at 0.878, has the lowest error consistency of the seven models compared (3.5% of the ceiling, against 7.3% for VGG-16 at shape bias 0.172; Supplementary Table S6). Across all 189 readouts of the w sweep the best κ never exceeds 20.7% of the ceiling, and equivalence intervals fixed in advance exclude a κ change larger than 0.038 across the 0.71 span of shape bias the readouts cover (Fig. 5b). Across the field's zoo agreement does rise with the rate, to at most 37% of the ceiling, but that rise is the measure's own bound: Cohen's κ cannot exceed a value fixed by the two marginals, one quadratic in the rate accounts for the agreement (R² = 0.90), and at a matched rate of 0.40 agreement separates neither the zoo's representations nor the Stylized-ImageNet network (four of six predictions of a fourth plan failed; Supplementary Table S13; Supplementary Note S4). The measure does detect a correspondence when one is there: the same machinery recovers 24.1% of the split-half ceiling on whether an image resolves to either cue at all, against 8.7% on which cue is picked (Fig. 5c, d).

**Representational dimensionality.** The magno/parvo ratio of the streams' participation ratios on NSD images orders the four front ends as a learning-free Gabor bank does, so it tracks the bandwidth a stream is handed; what the diet decides is which side of parity training ends on — every diet-trained seed ends magno-dominant, including the `parvo_highsf` front end, which we had pre-registered would stay inverted, while every no-diet seed collapses to parity (Extended Data Fig. 2). `parvo_highsf`'s second stream becomes the more dimensional stream while remaining texture-leaning (probe −0.139 on held-out exemplars), which separates dimensionality from shape bias.

## 3. Discussion

**The two questions.** The thesis asked whether magnocellular- and parvocellular-driven streams inside one network could diverge while the network behaves shape-biased, and whether such a system bears on why the ventral stream can be texture-like while behaviour is not. To the first the answer is yes: divergence requires the developmental transform on an achromatic carrier, and the texture-biased component is built by learning. To the second it is an existence proof: the texture-biased stream carries extrastriate cortex while behaviour, reading out from both, is shape-biased. Read as claims about one representation the two conflict; read as claims about a readout over components, they do not.

**What the readout account licenses.** A model matching the human shape-bias rate is routinely described as having human-like representations; here that inference fails twice. The same network holds components at +0.34 and −0.17 on held-out exemplars and behaves at 0.675, and one scalar over frozen representations moves the rate across most of the range the field's models occupy, so the rate does not identify a representation; and trial-by-trial agreement with the observers never exceeds 21% of the human–human ceiling in our networks, is lowest for our most shape-biased stream, and rises across the zoo only as far as the bound on κ allows, so the rate is at best a weak proxy for the decision process. Prior work established that a representation can hold more shape than its behaviour reveals (Hermann et al., 2020; Islam et al., 2021), that the measured rate depends on the test design (Tartaglini et al., 2022) and on a prompt in vision–language models (Gavrikov et al., 2024), and that it tracks a network's spatial-frequency channel (Subramanian et al., 2023); new here is the form of the dissociation — both representations held fixed, the rate moved by the readout alone — and its cortical grounding: the same mixing weight, fitted to cortex, prefers the magno-heavy end in V1 and the parvo-heavy end beyond it (Extended Data Fig. 5). Nor does the account need the split: in networks with none a gain on their own shape- and texture-predictive subspaces moves the rate on unseen exemplars and re-orders the zoo, and the texture-leaning units of our single trunks again carry extrastriate cortex (Fig. 6); the split amplifies the dissociation (0.50 against 0.25 on the same network), it does not create it.

**How to report shape bias.** A shape-bias claim should carry three numbers, each answering a different question: the rate at the trained readout (an operating point); the span the same frozen features reach under a reciprocal gain on their own shape- and texture-predictive subspaces within an accuracy budget (how much of the rate the readout controls); and a probe's shape-minus-texture accuracy (what the representation holds) — the last two on held-out exemplars, since the standard set reuses 48 texture images across 1,200 stimuli and exemplar-shared estimates inflate both. The probe's preprocessing must be fixed too, because with a few hundred training images it sets the probe's size: we fix exemplar-balanced leave-one-exemplar-out folds with PCA and scaling fitted inside each fold and an untuned C = 1 (Methods 4.3), and read the probe for sign and order, since the ImageNet median runs from −0.14 to −0.39 across four other held-out preprocessings (+0.03 for one that standardises components fitted on all images) while every one puts the Stylized-ImageNet network above all 38 ImageNet networks. Across 38 ImageNet networks the three rank the zoo differently (τ = 0.63, and 0.43 for the probe, 0.28–0.54 across preprocessings); error consistency, which at a network's own readout largely re-expresses the rate, should be read at a matched rate (Supplementary Table S13). None of these numbers alone licenses a claim about representations. One command in the repository prints all three, with the exemplar-shared values beside them, for any torchvision network (Methods 4.8).

**What a developmental constraint does, and what it costs.** The intuitive account of low-pass developmental input is informational — coarse input shields the learner from texture, so it learns shape — and our results do not support it: the transform removes bandwidth rather than adding structure, most of the shape bias it produces is present before training, and what the diet decides is whether learning builds on that bias or destroys it. It is a constraint on the solution learning converges to, and an expensive one — less accurate (0.556 against 0.701), less stable late in training, less robust and a worse predictor of BOLD — so nothing here recommends it as a training method; its interest is as a model of development.

**Limitations.** The load-bearing cortical result rests on eight subjects from one dataset and is read as sign and ordering only. The mapping between our chromatic stream and texture-selective cortex is by explanatory role, not content: nothing here asserts that the ventral stream is chromatic, the prediction that the chromatic stream should win in colour-biased cortex fails (pooled VO +0.005, bounded within ±0.017), and substituting the koniocellular axis makes it worse (Supplementary Note S2). The diet is a fixed 48-month front end rather than a trajectory. Whether a two-stream architecture specialises on its own is untestable here, because the trunks are bit-identical at initialisation. Human comparisons are against ten published observers and of aggregate rate; the readout weight is a hyperparameter that nothing selects. The cue-conflict set reuses its exemplars, so spans and probes are reported with exemplars held out (about 60% and two thirds of their exemplar-shared values in the zoo); the unit rule's η² split, fitted on all images, has not been recalibrated this way. Seven plans were committed before their analyses ran, and every outcome is reported; their ledgers, failures included, are summarised in Supplementary Note S5. The sixth, re-estimating spans and probes with exemplars held out, is post hoc with respect to the five before it, as are the held-out accuracy gate, the reversal floors and the probe's preprocessing spread added to it after a later referee simulation. Of the main study's four pre-registered predictions, two failed and are reported as failures; claims deliberately not made are listed in Supplementary Note S1.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Front end

RGB images are converted to the DKL cone-opponent space through RGB→XYZ (D65)→LMS (Hunt–Pointer–Estévez)→DKL at 256 × 256 px, each channel normalised to [0, 1] with the training set's global range and quantised to 8 bits exactly as the training data were stored (Derrington et al., 1984). Stream 1 receives the luminance channel; under the diet it passes through an isotropic Gaussian blur (σ = 4 px, kernel 33, reflect padding) and the scale-free contrast-sensitivity cut of Lu et al. (2026) evaluated at a fixed age of 48 months (`cs_logspan_start` = 5 × 10⁻³, decomposition mode “keep”). Stream 2 receives the L−M channel untransformed. The four conditions are: `dkl_bcs` (diet: luminance + blur + CS | L−M); `dkl_base` (no diet: luminance | L−M); `dkl_bcs_swap` (luminance | L−M + blur + CS); `dkl_bcs_parvo_highsf` (luminance + blur + CS | L−M stacked with untransformed luminance, two channels). The last differs architecturally in its first-layer fan-in, and that difference is measured rather than argued away (Extended Data Fig. 2; Results 2.5). Evaluation stimuli, including NSD images and cue-conflict images, pass through the identical front end.

### 4.2 Architecture and training

Two ResNet-18 trunks (He et al., 2016), one per stream, initialised bit-identically, with a joint linear classification head over the weighted sum of their penultimate features (w = 0.5; cross-stream mixing off). MiniEcoset at 256 px, 112 classes (Thorat & Kietzmann, 2024). 150 epochs, batch 256, Adam (lr 1 × 10⁻⁴, β₁ = 0.9; Kingma & Ba, 2017), cross-entropy, with the augmentation set of Lu et al. (2026) applied on GPU (horizontal flip, rotation ±15°, colour jitter, random equalise, perspective, sharpness; random grayscale for RGB inputs only). Three seeds per condition; weights saved every 25 epochs. Each run used one NVIDIA RTX A5000 (24 GB); the scheduler allocated the 21 training runs behind this paper 584 GPU-hours in total (an earlier draft gave 578, a sum of the runs' own timing logs that no committed file reproduces; the scheduler's figure below replaces it). All encoding, statistical and plotting work ran on CPU. Over the whole project the scheduler allocated 29,000 core-hours and 605 GPU-hours to 1,222 jobs between 2026-08-25 and 2026-09-17: 4,700 core-hours and 584 GPU-hours to the 21 training runs, 20 GPU-hours to the accuracy-matched retraining, 14,800 core-hours to the two-band encoding fits and their controls, 4,600 to the subspace-rule cortex fits, 2,000 to the single-trunk generality analysis, 980 to the cortex-optimal readout weight, 55 to the model zoo and 1,800 to smoke tests, caches, figures, the robustness runs and the verifier (`tmp_analysis/compute_accounting.py` reads the scheduler's own accounting and writes `results/figures_v2/compute_accounting.json`). The same script run from 2026-09-16 to 2026-09-23 (`results/figures_v2/compute_accounting_after.json`) adds 1,830 core-hours and 1.5 GPU-hours for the revision analyses, including the exemplar re-analysis and the koniocellular seeds, together with work not reported here; they are not included in the estimate below, and would raise it by less than 7%. Estimated with the Green Algorithms method (Lannelongue et al., 2021) at 12 W per allocated core, 0.3725 W per allocated gigabyte, the A5000's 230 W, a power usage effectiveness of 1.67 and Germany's grid intensity of 0.35 kg CO₂e per kWh, that is 930 kWh and 325 kg CO₂e. It is an upper bound: allocated cores and memory are counted as fully used for the whole of each job's wall-clock time. Not included are the pretraining of the public networks analysed in Results 2.4 and the acquisition of the two human datasets. Single-stream controls use one ResNet-18 or one ResNet-34 trunk fed the diet's luminance input with the identical schedule. The accuracy-matched control retrained `dkl_base` for 20 epochs with per-epoch checkpointing at three seeds and selected by rule, per seed, the epoch whose validation top-1 is nearest the diet arm's best-checkpoint accuracy of 0.556 (epochs 10, 10 and 11: 0.548, 0.546 and 0.554). No hyperparameter search was run for any arm: every condition uses the training recipe of Lu et al. (2026) unchanged, and the only selection is the checkpoint policy stated above.

### 4.3 Behaviour and probe

Cue-conflict shape bias is computed on the Geirhos et al. (2018a) style-transfer set restricted to the 16 categories present in MiniEcoset and to images whose shape and texture categories differ (1,200 images). Logits are restricted to the 16 columns; the argmax is scored as a shape or texture response; images decided as neither are excluded from the denominator and their count is reported. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals are over images. A second, forced two-way estimator keeps every image and scores whether the logit of the image's shape category exceeds that of its texture category; it is reported beside the primary estimator for every readout (Supplementary Table S9) and is computed from the same stored logits. The readout is full = classifier(w·y₁ + (1−w)·y₂), magno_only = classifier(w·y₁), parvo_only = classifier((1−w)·y₂), all through the same classifier and bias term; the w sweep evaluates full at 21 values of w on stored features. The refitted heads (Linear(512, 112) and Linear(512, 512)→ReLU→Linear(512, 112)) were trained on w·F₁ + (1−w)·F₂ of MiniEcoset training features at each w with an identical budget (Adam, lr 10⁻³, batch 1024, 20 epochs) and the same initialisation seed at every w. Human observers are the ten of the same release (1,280 trials each), scored by the identical rule; our recomputation reproduces the published 0.958 and the published AlexNet, GoogLeNet and VGG-16 values. Error consistency is Cohen's κ (Cohen, 1960) on the binary shape/texture choice over images both parties decided, with expected agreement p_m·p_h + (1−p_m)(1−p_h), reported as a percentage of the human–human κ over all 45 observer pairs (Geirhos et al., 2020). For the frozen networks of Methods 4.8 the same κ was computed at every gain w (`src/analysis/generality/kappa_sweep.py`), together with the largest κ attainable at the two marginals, κ_max = (1 − |p_m − p_h| − p_e)/(1 − p_e), against which agreement is also reported as κ/κ_max, as a percentage of the same ratio for human pairs (0.353). Per-image correspondence uses the observers' per-image shape rate and cue-resolution rate (split-half reliabilities r = .658 and .794 after category removal), the models' shape-minus-texture logit margin, w chosen on half the images and scored on the held-out half, as a percentage of each target's own ceiling. The linear probe reduces each stream's features to 256 PCA components and fits multinomial logistic regression (C = 1.0) under 5-fold stratified cross-validation to shape and texture labels separately; bias is shape accuracy minus texture accuracy on the same folds. Because the 1,200 images are built from 160 object and 48 texture exemplars, stratified folds leave every test image's exemplars in the training folds, so the primary probe is exemplar-balanced: three exemplars per category for both labels (three of each shape category's ten, drawn at random; the texture set has three), texture images subsampled to the same per-category count, scored leave one exemplar out with PCA (min(256, n_train − 1) components) and scaling fitted inside each fold, mean of 20 draws with the 5–95% interval over draws (`src/analysis/generality/exemplar.py`; `docs/PLAN_EXEMPLAR_2026-09-21.md`). The stratified-fold probe is reported beside it as exemplar-shared. With about 250 training images per fold the balanced probe is sensitive to preprocessing: PCA fitted on all images and then standardised compresses every bias toward zero (median +0.03 over the 38 ImageNet networks), whereas fewer components or no standardisation agree in sign with the primary estimator (medians −0.14 and −0.39; ρ = 0.94 with the latter over the 42 zoo networks). The reporting standard (Discussion) fixes the primary preprocessing, and the spread over the five held-out variants is reported beside it (`tmp_analysis/figs_v2/exemplar_probe_spread.py`).

### 4.4 Effective dimensionality

Participation ratio (Σλ)²/Σλ² of the stream feature matrix on 1,000 NSD images per subject (Gao et al., 2017), computed Gram-side, reported as the magno/parvo ratio with parity at 1.0. Subjects are the replication unit within a seed; seeds are reported separately and never pooled. A learning-free reference is the same ratio computed on a Gabor oriented-energy bank applied to each stream's own transformed input.

### 4.5 Encoding

NSD (Allen et al., 2022) `betas_fithrf_GLMdenoise_RR` single-trial betas, converted to percent signal change and z-scored per session per voxel, `nsdgeneral` mask, eight subjects. Splits are by 73k image id with the shared-515 images held out. Stream features for each subject's stimuli are reduced by PCA to 1,000 components per stream (fit on training images only) and entered as two bands into a banded ridge regression (himalaya; Dupré la Tour et al., 2022) with 3-fold GroupKFold by image id, per-voxel alphas and per-voxel band weights. Unique variance U_m (U_p) is the drop in cross-validated r² when the magno (parvo) band is removed. Noise ceilings are NSD's `ncsnr`; voxels below 0.1 are excluded and any ROI cell with fewer than 20 surviving voxels is reported as missing. ROIs are the `prf-visualrois` labels of the Wang et al. (2015) atlas as distributed with NSD (V1v, V1d, V2v, V2d, V3v, V3d, hV4), with hierarchical rank [1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4]. The canonical-correlation spectrum between the two bands runs from ≈0 to 0.93, so each band retains directions the other cannot predict and unique variance is identifiable; a recovery simulation with the fitted feature geometry and a known across-ROI split returns the true ordering exactly (ρ = +1.000 in 8/8 subjects, both arms), which is why the gradient is reported as sign and ordering rather than as calibrated magnitude. The swap and `parvo_highsf` arms are fitted for all three seeds, as the diet and no-diet arms are, and their behavioural and probe measures use the same three seeds (Supplementary Table S3). Both are degenerate by construction and in every seed — the swapped network leaves the parvo band a median unique variance 0.005–0.010 of the magno band's, the parvo + high-SF network leaves the magno band 0.003–0.005 of the parvo band's — so they enter no cortical claim beyond the sign and size of their unique variances (Results 2.3; Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Table S10). The fits of this section were originally submitted at about 160 GB of memory per subject, which is not the size of the data: the peak is the group-ridge random search, which forms an (n_alphas × n_test × n_train) array per gamma sample and fold, about 30 GB in float64 with all 21 alphas at once. Batching that search seven alphas at a time changes no estimate — a refit of the diet seed-0 subject-01 fit reproduces the stored one at voxelwise r = 1.00000 on both unique variances, with the largest per-ROI index difference below 10⁻⁴ — and the same fits then peak at 32.6 GB (`slurm/encode_equiv_probe.sh`, `slurm/encode_cc_controls.sh`). Eccentricity bands are NSD's `prf-eccrois` labels (0–0.5°, 0.5–1°, 1–2°, 2–4°, > 4°); continuous pRF eccentricity, size and R² are NSD's population-receptive-field estimates in the same functional space, and the within-subject regressions of Supplementary Table S8 use z-scored log(eccentricity + 0.5) and log(size) over voxels with pRF R² > 5%. The per-voxel predicted shape-bias index is obtained by pushing the cue-conflict set through each fitted encoding model. For Extended Data Fig. 4 and Table 11 the same voxelwise fits were relabelled, without refitting, with the full Kastner2015 atlas as distributed with NSD (adding VO1/2, PHC1/2, LO1/2, TO1/2, V3A/B and IPS0–2; IPS3–5, SPL1 and FEF have no voxels inside `nsdgeneral`), with NSD's `streams` parcellation and with the fLoc face, place, body and word ROIs; regional means are unweighted means of the ROI medians, and region contrasts are tested per subject with the exact Wilcoxon test. For the cortex-optimal readout weight (Extended Data Fig. 5; Extended Data Table 2) the classifier's own input z(w) = w·y₁ + (1 − w)·y₂, where y₁ and y₂ are the 512-dimensional stream outputs the classifier mixes, was the design of a single-band ridge model of every `nsdgeneral` voxel for w ∈ {0, 0.05, …, 1}, with the same split, grouped folds and alpha grid as the banded model and columns z-scored on the training trials; because z(w) sums the streams dimension by dimension the ridge cannot undo the mixture, so w is a constraint on the design rather than a re-parameterisation of the two-band model. w*_cortex is the w that maximises the median test r² over an ROI's reliable voxels (seeds averaged per subject); the 1,024-dimensional concatenation [y₁, y₂] is fitted as a reference and not as a bound, because although every mixture is a linear map of it the two models are regularised separately on a shared alpha grid, and the best mixture exceeds the fitted concatenation in 36 of the 112 subject × ROI × arm cells by a median 0.0019 r² — more than the 0.0016 the sweep itself buys from w = ½ to w*, so w*_cortex is read as the location of an optimum and never as an r² difference (Extended Data Table 2); and the behaviour-optimal weight is the trial-fit w of Fig. 4 (Methods 4.3) for each of the ten observers, reported with its per-fit bootstrap interval and permutation p and not compared with w*_cortex, because it is not identified (Results 2.4). The koniocellular control substitutes S−(L+M) for L−M in stream 2 of the untrained network at each of three seeds, each against the L−M network of its own seed, contrast-matched to L−M per image, with the magno band bit-identical across arms (gate max|Δr²_magno| < 10⁻⁴, passed at 0.000).

### 4.6 Out-of-distribution robustness

Corruptions are applied to RGB before the front end and scored in the native 112-class label space. Five families were declared untrained-on before the run (uniform noise, high-pass, phase scrambling, rotation, false colour) and two as trained-in positive controls (low-pass, contrast). Reported as the area under the relative-accuracy curve across severities, at each seed's best saved checkpoint. Three implementation differences from Lu et al. (2026) are declared: our front end is evaluated at a fixed 48 months rather than scheduled (the one we can rule out, since a schedule-trained arm behaves like the static diet); our diet is a transform on one stream's input of a two-stream network rather than an augmentation on a single trunk; and we train on MiniEcoset and score corruptions in that label space, applied before the front end.

### 4.7 Statistics

Within-subject comparisons use the paired exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 8 (exact two-sided floor p = .0078); seed-level comparisons use the exact Mann–Whitney U, 3 v 3 (floor p = 0.100, reached only on complete separation), and seeds are never pooled with subjects. Every cortical contrast is computed both on the raw per-voxel difference U_m − U_p and on the normalised index (U_m − U_p)/(U_m + U_p), each summarised by its median over reliable voxels per ROI, and a claim is made only where the two agree; disagreements are reported as unresolved. Equivalence uses two one-sided tests with bounds fixed before any interval was computed (Schuirmann, 1987; Lakens, 2017). No multiple-comparison correction is claimed: with 28 p-values and an exact floor of .0078, Holm–Bonferroni over any family larger than six cannot yield a rejection, so claims rest on complete separation, agreement of both statistics, replication across seeds and effect size (dz = −6.60 for the diet gradient against −1.09 without the diet). Behavioural and robustness results use each seed's best saved checkpoint unless stated; cortical fits use the final checkpoint. Four predictions — the front-end 2×2, the inverted-front-end prediction, the corruption families and the error-consistency hypothesis — were written into a version-controlled plan and committed before the corresponding analysis was run (internal pre-specification, not registry-based); two failed and are reported as failures.

### 4.8 Readout re-weighting in networks without a stream split

Pre-specified three times — in `docs/PLAN_GENERALITY_2026-09-14.md` (the unit-level rule; five directional predictions P1–P5), `docs/PLAN_SUBSPACE_2026-09-15.md` (the subspace rule; predictions P-A, P-A′, P-B, P-C and P-D) and `docs/PLAN_ZOO_2026-09-15.md` (the model zoo; P-Z1–P-Z4) — each committed before its analysis was run, with every outcome reported (Results 2.4; Extended Data Tables 3 and 4; Supplementary Tables S11 and S12). Both rules act on a frozen network with logits = W·y + b, y its D-dimensional penultimate features, and neither retrains anything. Subspace rule: on a fitting set of cue-conflict images y is centred and reduced by PCA to min(256, D) components; a 16-class linear discriminant analysis on the shape labels (scikit-learn, eigen solver, Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage) gives 15 shape-discriminant directions and one on the texture labels 15 texture-discriminant directions; mapped back to unit space and orthonormalised, the two bases are made mutually orthogonal by symmetric (Löwdin) orthogonalisation, which treats shape and texture identically (sequential Gram–Schmidt in either order is the reported sensitivity; Extended Data Table 3), giving B_S, B_T and an orthonormal basis B_R of the residual, with projectors P_S, P_T and P_R. Fixed-head gain sweep: logits(w) = W·(P_R y + 2w·P_S y + 2(1 − w)·P_T y) + b, w ∈ {0, 0.05, …, 1}, so that w = 0.5 is the trained network exactly; the bases are fitted on one half of the 1,200 images, stratified by shape × texture pair, and the rate scored on the other, over 10 random splits in both directions. These halves share exemplars (98% of scored images share their object exemplar with the fitting half and all of them their texture exemplar), so every span is also computed on exemplar-disjoint folds (`docs/PLAN_EXEMPLAR_2026-09-21.md`, predictions P-X1–P-X16, committed before its code existed): in each of ten repetitions every texture category's three exemplars and every shape category's ten object exemplars are assigned at random to three folds, the bases are fitted on the images whose object and texture exemplars both lie outside fold k and the rate is scored on the images whose exemplars both lie inside it, and the three folds' scored images (about 400) form one curve; holding out only the texture or only the object exemplars gives the two components (median spans 0.195 and 0.293 over the 38 ImageNet networks, against 0.19 for both and 0.30 for neither). As for the exemplar-shared estimator, the reported span is max − min of the mean curve over the gated w; the pre-registered predictions were scored on the mean of the repetitions' own spans (median 0.189 against 0.187). The ranking's noise floor is the fraction of pairs reversing between two independent bootstrap resamples of the 1,200 images at the trained readouts (2,000 pairs of resamples, shared across networks). Unit-level rule, the special case in which the two subspaces are spanned by disjoint sets of units: each unit j receives d_j = η²_shape(j) − η²_texture(j), the one-way fraction of its variance over the images lying between the 16 shape or the 16 texture categories; the median split of d gives the shape-leaning half S and the texture-leaning half T (equal-sized, as the two streams are), and logits(w) = W·(g(w) ⊙ y) + b with g_j = 2w for j ∈ S and 2(1 − w) for j ∈ T, on the same image splits. At a referee's request, and not pre-specified, this rule was also run with S the absolute shape-leaning set (units with d_j > 0) or the top decile of units by d_j, T the remainder (Supplementary Table S11, last two columns; `slurm/generality_rules.sh`): both leave S small — 2–89 units under the absolute rule (a single unit, hence undefined, for ConvNeXt-S) and a tenth of the units (51–409) under the decile rule — so that w → 1 silences at least nine tenths of the representation; the ungated spans are 0.11–0.40 with positive mean ρ (0.29–0.99) but not every split is monotone in our trunks, and within 5 points of the trained top-1 — a gate that closes at w = 0.72–0.95 in every case but ConvNeXt-B under the decile rule, where the whole sweep passes — the reachable span is 0.02–0.21. Estimators: our networks by Methods 4.3; torchvision ImageNet networks (ResNet-50, ResNet-152, VGG-16, ViT-B/16, ConvNeXt-S/B, IMAGENET1K_V1 weights, model-vs-human preprocessing) by the Geirhos 16-class rule, mean softmax probability over each category's ImageNet classes and argmax over the 16, which assigns every image to one of the 16 categories; as in Methods 4.3, the rate is computed over the images assigned to their shape or their texture category (814 of 1,200 for ResNet-50; per-network counts in Extended Data Table 4). Accuracy gate: top-1 of the gained features, with the bases or the split defined on all 1,200 images, on held-out natural images — the MiniEcoset validation split for our networks and a stratified 10-per-class subset of the ILSVRC-2012 validation set for the ImageNet networks; the gated span is max − min of the seed-mean held-out curve over the w at which top-1 lies within 5 points of the trained value. For the exemplar-disjoint spans of the 42 zoo networks the gate is recomputed on the same fold bases the spans use (post hoc, `docs/PLAN_EXEMPLAR_2026-09-21.md` addendum C; `src/analysis/generality/exemplar_gate.py`, `results/figures_v2/exemplar_gate.json`): top-1 is averaged over the 30 fold bases at each w. Under those bases the largest top-1 change over the sweep is a median 1.5 times that on the all-image bases (up to 6.3 points against 4.4), the gate narrows in three networks (ResNet-18 to w = 0.10–0.95, MobileNet-V2 and the Stylized-ImageNet ResNet-50 to 0.05–0.95; four with ResNet-34 if every fold basis must stay within budget), and the zoo summaries do not change (gated span 0.08–0.29, median 0.18, 11 of 38 ≥ 0.20; 19% of pairs reversed, τ = 0.63). The six networks of Fig. 6 keep the whole sweep inside the gate (largest change 3.5 points, against 1.5 on the all-image bases). Our trunks' gate stays on the all-image bases, because their MiniEcoset validation features were not stored. Model zoo (Extended Data Fig. 6, Extended Data Table 4): the same pipeline on 42 networks — 38 torchvision `IMAGENET1K_V1` classifiers, ResNet-50 under torchvision's `IMAGENET1K_V2` recipe, and the three ResNet-50s of Geirhos et al. (2018a) trained on Stylized-ImageNet, on Stylized-ImageNet plus ImageNet, and on both then fine-tuned on ImageNet — with y the input of each network's last linear layer, captured on the network's own forward pass and checked against its logits; Resize 256 / CenterCrop 224 except Inception-v3 (342/299), EfficientNet-B3 (320/300) and EfficientNet-V2-S (384/384), which were not trained at 224. The probe bias is the linear probe of Methods 4.3 on the same 1,200 features, predicting the shape and the texture class in turn, under both of its estimators. Ranking statistics over the 38 ImageNet-trained networks are Kendall τ between rankings and the fraction of the 703 pairs whose order reverses, with no p-values, the networks being a fixed set rather than a sample. `python -m src.analysis.generality.report --model tv:<arch>` prints the three numbers for any torchvision classifier. Refit: because a refitted linear head can undo a per-direction gain, the refit test mixes the two components lossily, as the two-stream model does, and passes the rest of the representation unchanged — z(w) = [w·s + (1 − w)·t, B_R^T^y] with s = B_S^T^y and t = B_T^T^y paired by discriminant rank (under the unit-level rule z(w) = w·y_S + (1 − w)·y_T, the halves paired by rank of d) — and refits the linear and two-layer heads of Methods 4.3 at each w, with a head refitted on the unmixed y as the accuracy ceiling. Cortex: under the unit-level rule S and T (split on all 1,200 images) are the two bands of the banded ridge model of Methods 4.5 on the penultimate units of the single-trunk networks, giving U_S, U_T and the index (U_S − U_T)/(U_S + U_T) per voxel; the cortex-optimal w of the lossy sum follows the protocol of Extended Data Fig. 5 on an 11-point grid (0, 0.1, …, 1). Under the subspace rule the bands are s = B_S^T^y, t = B_T^T^y and the residual r = B_R^T^y (15, 15 and D − 30 dimensions; bases fitted on all 1,200 images), with U_S = r²_{STR} − r²_{TR} and U_T = r²_{STR} − r²_{SR} from the three-band model and the two two-band models (random search with 30 and 20 samples), and the w sweep on the 15-dimensional lossy sum w·s + (1 − w)·t alone; the five families are `rgb_tsn` and `dkl_bcs_single34` (three seeds each, averaged per subject), ResNet-50, and ViT-B/16 and ConvNeXt-B, whose NSD features were extracted for this test (predictions P-C1–P-C3; Supplementary Table S12, upper block). The `rgb_tsn` network — two trunks that receive the identical three-channel image and remain bit-identical — is one ResNet-18 on the untransformed image and is used as the no-diet single trunk. All of our networks enter this analysis at their final checkpoints, the policy of the cortical fits (Methods 4.7).

Evidence decomposition (post hoc; `docs/PLAN_SPAN_2026-09-18.md`, predictions S-1 to S-7 written before the analysis; Supplementary Table S14). For image i with shape category s and texture category t the Geirhos rule's margin is Δ_i = log p_s − log p_t, p_c the mean softmax probability over c's ImageNet classes, and its gradient with respect to the logits is u_i = q_s − q_t, q_c the softmax restricted to c's classes. Because logits(w) = logits(½) + (2w − 1)·W(P_S − P_T)y exactly, e_S,i = u_i·W P_S y_i and e_T,i = u_i·W P_T y_i give the first-order margin Δ_i + (2w − 1)(e_S,i − e_T,i) (model M0) and, with z = mean Δ / sd Δ and λ = mean(e_S − e_T) / sd Δ over the 1,200 images, the two-number forced rate Φ(z + (2w − 1)λ) (M1); the shape term is mean e_S, the texture term −mean e_T and the texture share the second over their sum. All use the bases fitted on all 1,200 images and are compared with the forced-choice span in sample. Null: ten pairs of mutually orthogonal random 15-dimensional subspaces (seeds 0–9) inside each network's 256-dimensional PCA space of the cue-conflict features, under the same gain, estimator and gate (`src/analysis/generality/span_model.py`, `slurm/zoo_span.sh`).

## Ethics and human participants

This study collected no new human data. Both human datasets are existing, de-identified public releases analysed under their own terms. NSD was collected under the approval of the University of Minnesota institutional review board with written informed consent from all eight participants (Allen et al., 2022); the behavioural data were collected under the approval described in Geirhos et al. (2018a). Secondary analysis of two de-identified public datasets required no additional approval under our institution's policy, and no participant-identifying information was accessed.

**Sex and demographics.** Participant sex was not under our control and is not a design factor; no analysis is disaggregated by sex, and at eight participants and ten observers none could be, because the exact tests used already sit at their resolution floor on the full samples. We do not restate sex or age: the Geirhos per-observer files carry only trial-level fields, so no per-observer demographic exists in the data we analysed, and for NSD we have not verified values against the released subject-information file and therefore point to the NSD release rather than transcribe from prose.

**Constraints on generality.** The neuroimaging sample is eight adults with normal or corrected-to-normal vision in the United States; the behavioural reference is ten adults tested in a German university laboratory. Both are small, Western, convenience-recruited adult samples. Claims about “human observers” should be read as claims about these samples; nothing here establishes cross-cultural, developmental or clinical generality, and the developmental motivation of the model's front end is a motivation for the model, not a claim about any developmental population we measured.

## Supplementary Note S1 — claims deliberately not made

- That the two-stream split is what makes shape bias a readout property: networks with no split show the same dissociation — a reciprocal gain on their shape- and texture-predictive subspaces moves the rate by 0.14–0.20 in six ImageNet networks within 3.5 points of top-1 on exemplars the subspaces never saw (0.25–0.33 with exemplars shared), and in our trunks extrastriate cortex loads on the texture-leaning half relative to V1 — with the cortical part failing under the unit-level rule in all three ImageNet networks fitted (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16 and ConvNeXt-B, the last two post hoc) (Fig. 6; Results 2.4).
- That the two-stream split causes a shape-bias advantage over a single stream: at matched capacity the arms do not separate at n = 3, and capacity alone moves the rate by a comparable amount (Results 2.4).
- Any dorsal/ventral stream mapping. The thesis's framing “magno→dorsal, parvo→ventral” was tested rather than dropped: the pre-declared dorsal−ventral interaction is +0.0245 on the diet model and +0.0122 (p = .195) without the low-pass front end, so it does not survive its own control. Read directly on the Wang atlas, dorsal (V3A/B, IPS0–2) and ventral (VO1/2, PHC1/2) regions are indistinguishable under both arms (dorsal − ventral +0.015 with the diet, +0.005 without, p > .1; Extended Data Fig. 4c): the diet's magno stream contributes no unique variance anywhere beyond V3.
- hMT+ specificity: at n = 8 the two statistics disagree (p = .195 normalised, .039 raw) and the effect is dead in the blur control.
- A foveal/peripheral headline: the foveal band's sign flips when the blur is removed (−0.057 → +0.051), so it is a front-end artefact.
- That the chromatic stream predicts colour-biased cortex: pooled VO is +0.0045 (p = .46), bounded within ±0.017; hV4's parvo preference is weak (−0.012, 2/8). Substituting the koniocellular axis makes the prediction worse, and worse specifically in the colour set (hV4 −0.029, VO −0.024, each p = .0078, 0/8).
- Where in cortex the diet's shape-bias effect lands: a diet-specific hV4/LO step does not survive the full control set.
- That integration produces human-like shape bias, or that the diet improves neural predictivity: it does neither.
- That the diet makes the model's image-level difficulty profile more human-like: the crossed 2×2 shows train/test matching, not a weights effect.
- That human cortex uses this mechanism: a single-stream account in which texture-like ventral representations feed shape-biased behaviour directly is not excluded by these data.

Why the three regional claims above failed together, and what it implies for the survivor: without the low-pass front end essentially every ROI is magno-preferring, so the blur manufactures apparent regional specificity by handicapping the magno stream until the parvo stream can win somewhere — the same general-factor pathology that defeated the thesis's RSA, reappearing in encoding form. Any regional claim on these models must be shown to survive removal of the blur, and the ventral gradient is reported because it does.

## Supplementary Note S2 — the koniocellular control

The thesis omitted the koniocellular pathway for simplicity. Because L−M fails to predict colour-biased cortex, two readings were live: that the chromatic stream does not predict colour cortex, or that L−M is the wrong chromatic axis. Swapping S−(L+M) into stream 2 of the untrained network, contrast-matched to L−M per image, with the luminance stream bit-identical, makes the ventral prediction worse and worse where colour lives (seed 0: hV4 −0.029, VO −0.024, the pre-declared colour set −0.026, each p = .0078 with 0/8 subjects positive on both statistics; colour set minus the other 17 ROIs −0.014, p = .0078), unchanged when the channel keeps its native 2.05× contrast, and replicated at two further untrained seeds, each against the L−M network of its own seed (colour set −0.027 and −0.020, colour set minus the other ROIs −0.016 and −0.014, every contrast p = .0078 with 0/8 subjects positive on both statistics; `results/konio_verdict/`). Both cone-opponent axes have now been tested against colour-biased cortex and neither predicts it. These fits are untrained, the native-contrast variant was run at seed 0 only, no third stream was added, and no blur was applied to the S channel; whether a three-band model beats a two-band one is a separate question requiring three-way variance partitioning.

## Supplementary Note S3 — the cortical gradient against its nuisance axes and the instrument's bias

Results 2.3 states the outcome; this note carries the analyses. See Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables S7 and S8.

**The two nuisance axes that co-vary with hierarchical rank.** Receptive-field size and eccentricity coverage both rise from V1 to hV4, and the diet's magno stream is a low-pass instrument by construction, so a stream-preference gradient that merely tracked receptive-field size would be expected from the front end alone. We tested this three ways (Extended Data Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S8). First, the gradient computed within each of NSD's five eccentricity bands — V1 and hV4 voxels compared only with voxels of the same eccentricity — is negative under the diet in every band (6/7 subjects in the foveal band, 8/8 in the other four) while the no-diet arm stays between −0.10 and +0.07 in every band; the diet arm's gradient does grow with eccentricity (−0.26 in the foveal band, −0.93 beyond 4°), so eccentricity modulates it, but the contrast is present at matched eccentricity and is therefore not an eccentricity contrast in disguise. Second, stratifying the same voxels by pRF size gives the same result within size tertiles (−0.71, −0.77 and −0.90 under the diet against −0.08 in each tertile without it). Third, regressing the per-voxel index on log pRF eccentricity and log pRF size jointly within subject (NSD's pRF estimates, voxels with pRF R² > 5%; the two predictors are only modestly collinear, VIF 1.66) shows that under the diet, stream preference moves toward parvo with increasing pRF size at fixed eccentricity (partial slope −0.086, 8/8 subjects, p = .0078) and toward magno with increasing eccentricity at fixed size (+0.119, 8/8, p = .0078), while the no-diet arm shows neither the size effect in that direction (+0.035, opposite sign) nor any eccentricity effect (−0.004, p = 1.00); the arm interaction on the eccentricity partial is +0.134 (8/8, p = .0078). A learning-free Gabor bank on the diet front end carries no such structure (+0.035, p = .15), so it is not a property of the input filter alone; an untrained network carries it on either front end, however (diet front end +0.083, 7/8, p = .016 on the eccentricity partial and −0.038, 6/8, p = .039 on the size partial; undieted front end +0.089, 7/8, p = .023 and −0.038, 8/8, p = .0078), and training with the diet leaves it where random weights put it — trained minus untrained is +0.036 (6/8, p = .11) on the normalised statistic and −0.077 (2/8, p = .11) on the raw one, opposite in sign and neither significant — while the network trained without the diet carries none of it on either statistic (+0.028 raw, −0.004 normalised; the paired decrease from the untrained network is significant on the raw statistic only, −0.374, 0/8, p = .0078, against −0.087, 2/8, p = .15). The diet preserves a dissociation that random convolutional weights already produce; it neither installs nor amplifies it (Supplementary Table S8). Within the diet arm, then, the ventral gradient and the pRF-size dependence are the same fact seen on two axes — larger receptive fields, further along the hierarchy, are increasingly explained by the texture-biased stream — and the network trained without the diet shows neither.

**The estimator's own bias points the wrong way.** The mean noise ceiling declines from V1v to hV4 (ρ = −0.89 against rank), a nuisance falling along the axis of the effect, so we simulated the instrument: synthetic voxels from each arm's fitted feature spaces, the measured noise ceilings imposed, the true magno − parvo difference set to zero everywhere. The recovered contrast is a small, positive floor (+0.038 with the diet's feature geometry, +0.051 without, 8/8 subjects each), flat across ROIs (+0.025 to +0.027 per ROI), and indistinguishable from zero on the raw statistic — opposite in sign to the observed effect and without its gradient. Because the floor is a signed bias, the right comparison is a paired displacement: the real contrast minus each subject's own floor is −0.871 with the diet and −0.130 without, each non-zero in every subject, the diet arm's 6.7 times larger (Supplementary Table S7). The floor subtraction relocates the recovery simulation's residual misfit rather than removing it, and we do not correct that residual, because the correction is under-specified: six variants swing the no-diet arm between 4/8 and 6/8 subjects and between p = .055 and p = 1.00, while the diet arm is non-zero in all eight subjects at p = .0078 under all six (Supplementary Table S7). The no-diet arm's non-zero gradient therefore rests on the floor correction alone, and since the simulation is not magnitude-faithful (it recovers the true contrast with a slope of 1.21 to 1.28), we read −0.834 as a sign-and-ordering result, not a calibrated effect size. The raw statistic per ROI is drawn beside the normalised one in Extended Data Fig. 3a; the diet arm's normalised index approaches −0.84 at hV4, near the index's bound, and the raw profile carries the same ordering without that bound.

**Introduction.**

Humans presented with an image whose shape and texture point to different categories name the shape: on the cue-conflict images of Geirhos et al. (2018a), ten observers did so on 96% of decided trials, while ImageNet-trained convolutional networks named the texture. Closing that gap has become a standard target for biologically motivated models (Hermann et al., 2020; Jang & Tong, 2021), and a developmental visual diet — training on input whose acuity, contrast sensitivity and chromatic sensitivity follow the human developmental trajectory — is among the most effective routes to it (Lu et al., 2026).

A second body of evidence sits uneasily beside the first: along the ventral stream, object representations are well described by texture statistics — category-selective human cortex fails to discriminate natural images from texture-matched scrambles yet still discriminates categories (Jagadeesh & Gardner, 2022), macaque V4 responses to natural textures are predicted by their image statistics (Okazawa et al., 2015), and in inferotemporal cortex texture is the more decodable cue (Jagadeesh & Livingstone, 2024; Komatsu & Goda, 2018). The premise is scoped — one human study with one manipulation, the rest macaque, and “texture-like” meaning that a texture-statistics description suffices, not that shape is absent — and the argument needs only that the ventral description is substantially texture-like.

If behaviour is shape-biased and the ventral stream texture-biased, either the two measures disagree about what shape bias is, or behaviour is not a readout of one representation. The second horn is a hypothesis about architecture that single-trunk models of the ventral stream cannot express, and two facts make it concrete: the visual system receives not one image but a luminance-dominated, spatially coarse magnocellular signal and a chromatically opponent, finer parvocellular one, separated before cortex and later recombined (Livingstone & Hubel, 1987; Kaplan & Shapley, 1982; Derrington et al., 1984; Nassi & Callaway, 2009; Ferrera et al., 1994), and that input is not stationary over development, so the system first learns from a low-passed world. The two-stream hypothesis (Mishkin et al., 1983; Goodale & Milner, 1992) and the emergence of ventral- and dorsal-like streams in a two-pathway network trained on one objective (Bakhtiari et al., 2021) motivate an architecture whose streams are defined by their subcortical-like inputs rather than by their cortical targets.

This paper asks two questions, in the order the originating study posed them (Manocchio, 2026): can two streams fed magnocellular- and parvocellular-like inputs diverge while optimising a single objective and producing shape-biased behaviour, and does such a system explain how the ventral stream can be texture-like while behaviour is shape-biased? We build it — two ResNet-18 trunks receiving the luminance and L−M channels of a cone-opponent image, with a developmental diet on the luminance channel (Fig. 1) — and examine it with linear probes and cue-conflict behaviour on the streams (Fig. 2), voxel-wise encoding models of eight subjects' 7T fMRI responses (Fig. 3) and the behavioural readout that combines the streams (Fig. 4). The streams diverge by different routes; the texture-biased one carries extrastriate cortex; and the integrated behaviour is shape-biased because a behavioural rate is a property of a readout over components, not a report of any one of them — a property that holds, in kind, in networks with no stream split at all (Fig. 6). Because the account predicts that properties bundled with shape bias need not travel with it, we test that too: robustness, agreement with human decisions and representational dimensionality all come apart from the rate (Fig. 5).

Relative to the originating thesis, every condition is trained with three seeds and every claim states its replication unit; the representational-similarity analysis that had found no stream-to-brain mapping is replaced by variance partitioning of encoding models, which removes the global stream preference that had defeated it; and the stream weighting the thesis fixed by hand is swept, so that the dependence of behaviour on the readout is measured rather than assumed.

**Results 2.1.**

The network splits its input once, before any convolution (Fig. 1a): each image is converted to the DKL cone-opponent space, stream 1 receives the luminance channel and stream 2 the L−M channel, and each is a ResNet-18 trunk. Under the developmental visual diet the luminance channel passes through a Gaussian blur (σ = 4 px) and the scale-free contrast-sensitivity cut of Lu et al. (2026) at a fixed age of 48 months, separating the transform from the trajectory (Methods 4.1). The trunks are initialised bit-identically — a control in which both streams receive the same image leaves all 120 tensor pairs identical after 150 epochs — so every stream difference below is attributable to the inputs, and a linear classifier reads out w·y₁ + (1−w)·y₂ of the two streams' penultimate features, with w = 0.5 during training.

The diet costs accuracy (Fig. 1c): the no-diet conditions converge to 0.70 validation top-1, the diet arm reaches 0.556 at its best saved checkpoint against 0.701 and, uniquely, degrades after its peak, ending at 0.445 (range 0.347–0.527), so behavioural and out-of-distribution results use each seed's best saved checkpoint, stated wherever the policy matters (Methods 4.7). Two single-stream controls trained with the identical diet — a ResNet-18 (11.2M parameters) and a ResNet-34 parameter-matched to the two-stream model (21.3M against 22.4M) — reach 0.599 and 0.623, so the accuracy cost of the split is not a capacity artefact (Fig. 4c).

**Results 2.2.**

Read out separately with a linear probe on the cue-conflict images, the two streams of the diet-trained network end on opposite sides of the shape–texture boundary: scored on exemplars the probe was not trained on, shape − texture accuracy is +0.337 for the magno stream and −0.168 for the parvo stream, means of three seeds, every seed on its side (Fig. 2a; +0.819 and −0.518 with exemplars shared; Methods 4.3); the magno stream decodes a new exemplar's texture at chance (0.08, against 0.0625). Behaviour agrees: read out alone at the final checkpoint, the policy of the cortical fits, the magno stream's cue-conflict shape bias is 0.878 and the parvo stream's 0.418, against 0.675 for the integrated readout (Table 1; best-checkpoint values in Supplementary Table S9) — the divergence the thesis reported on a single model per condition, now replicated across seeds.

The decomposition arises asymmetrically (Fig. 2a, Table 1). Scored on the untrained network with the same front end, most of the magno stream's shape bias is present before any weight is updated — the low-pass and contrast-sensitivity transform does it — at +0.210 of the trained +0.337 on held-out exemplars; the parvo stream begins nearly unbiased (−0.033) and acquires its texture preference over training. The shape-biased component starts in the input, the texture-biased component is built by learning, and what ordinary training does to the input's contribution is the decisive contrast: with the transform removed, the untrained luminance stream starts lower (+0.090) and training drives it across the sign flip to −0.122 — it does not merely fail to become shape-biased, it becomes texture-leaning — while the parvo stream ends texture-leaning whether or not the other stream is dieted (−0.168 against −0.118). Nor does the dieted input keep its bias alone: a single ResNet-18 trained on the magno stream's exact input and recipe ends at a rate of 0.557 and a penultimate-layer probe of −0.06 to −0.10 on held-out exemplars, where the same input as one of two streams ends at 0.878 and +0.26 to +0.27 — the shape-biased component survives training beside a second stream.

Which property of the input supplies the shape bias? Crossing the carrier a stream receives (achromatic luminance or chromatic L−M) with whether the developmental transform is applied to it gives four cells, two of which the swap and `parvo_highsf` conditions provide (Fig. 2b, c): on the probe, scored on held-out exemplars, the chromatic carrier gives −0.118 without the transform and −0.002 with it, the achromatic carrier −0.139 without and +0.337 with, so only the conjunction crosses zero, with an interaction of +0.36 over the additive prediction of the other three cells (−0.02) and no seed overlap between the conjunction cell (+0.333 to +0.340) and any other (at most +0.008); with exemplars shared the interaction is +0.73, and cue-conflict shape bias on the same four cells replicates it (Supplementary Table S3). The shape bias requires the developmental transform applied specifically to an achromatic channel — a relational fact about what a stream receives, not a property of a stream called “magno”.

Two qualifications belong here. First, cue-conflict shape bias is computed only over images a readout decides as the shape or the texture category, a count that varies four-fold across cells (165 to 598 of 1,200; Fig. 2d), so the three non-conjunction behavioural cells are read as “no strong preference”; the probe, which classifies every image, gives the same pattern, and so does a forced two-way estimator over every image, which compresses values toward 0.5 (the four cells read 0.44, 0.51, 0.35 and 0.76) while preserving every ordering this paper draws a conclusion from, the two no-preference cells trading places (Supplementary Table S9). Second, the diet-trained models are less accurate: we pre-registered the fatal outcome — a no-diet model at the diet's accuracy with a shape-biased magno stream — and retrained the no-diet network with per-epoch checkpointing, selecting by rule, in each of three seeds, the epoch nearest the diet's accuracy (0.546–0.554 against 0.556); matching accuracy closes 4.3% of the probe gap on held-out exemplars (1.5–5.9% across seeds; 7.2% with exemplars shared) and 6.1% of the behavioural gap (2.2–9.1%), and the matched magno stream stays on the texture side in every seed (−0.10 to −0.12; Fig. 2e, f).

**Results 2.3.**

The thesis had asked whether the streams align with different cortical streams by representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008) against seven regions of the Natural Scenes Dataset, and found that whichever stream was the more predictive correlated better with every region — a general factor that a correlation cannot separate from regional structure. We replaced the instrument with voxel-wise banded-ridge encoding models for each of the eight NSD subjects, one band per stream, partitioning every voxel's cross-validated explained variance into the part only the magno band accounts for (U_m) and the part only the parvo band does (U_p) (Methods 4.5); the per-voxel index (U_m − U_p)/(U_m + U_p), median over reliable voxels per ROI, across V1v → hV4 is Fig. 3a.

The two trained models sit on opposite sides of the global stream-preference axis — without the diet every retinotopic area is magno-explained, with it every area is parvo-explained — and that global difference, the general factor that defeated the correlational analysis, is not the result. The result is the shape of the profile: only the diet arm descends steeply from V1 to hV4, early visual cortex relatively more magno-explained and hV4 relatively more parvo-explained, the direction a luminance/low-spatial-frequency versus chromatic split predicts, and the descent belongs to the model whose global preference points the other way, which a model simply preferring one stream everywhere could not produce.

Quantified as the contrast hV4 − mean(V1v, V1d) of the per-ROI median (Fig. 3b; Extended Data Table 1), the gradient is −0.745 to −0.834 across the three diet-trained seeds against −0.049 to −0.096 without the diet, with the same separation on the raw statistic U_m − U_p, and all six comparisons reach the exact Wilcoxon floor for eight subjects (p = .0078) with complete seed separation. The per-subject Spearman ρ between hierarchical rank and stream preference is −0.964 to −0.973 with the diet and −0.46 to −0.66 without (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table S1); the rank vector [1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4] admits exactly 630 labellings of the seven ROIs, and against that enumerable null — which calibrates the extremity of a result that was not pre-specified rather than testing a prediction — the diet arm's ρ is the single most extreme in every seed and on both statistics (p = 1/630 = .0016) while the no-diet arm is not (p = .019 to .092), so we claim nothing there.

The diet does not create the gradient; it decides what training does to the one the input supplies. An untrained network on the diet front end already sits at −0.544 (8/8 subjects, p = .0078), and on the no-diet front end at almost the same value (−0.479), so the two front ends alone impose no different gradient; training with the diet steepens it and training without it erodes it to near zero (Fig. 3b). Correcting each arm for the signed bias of the estimator itself — a simulated floor of the wrong sign, +0.04 with the diet and +0.05 without — leaves a paired displacement of −0.871 with the diet against −0.130 without, each non-zero in every subject, and an independently trained checkpoint from the thesis's own pipeline, with its progressive developmental schedule, gives −0.793.

Pushed through models fitted on natural scenes only, the cue-conflict images yield a per-voxel predicted shape-bias index whose magno − parvo difference is +1.53 to +1.57 with the diet against +0.08 to +0.11 without (8/8 subjects, p = .0078 in every seed; Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table S2) — a test that needs neither probe nor readout.

**The gradient survives its nuisance axes and the instrument's own bias.** Receptive-field size, eccentricity coverage and the noise ceiling all change from V1 to hV4, and the diet's magno stream is a low-pass instrument by construction; yet the diet gradient is negative within every one of NSD's five eccentricity bands and every pRF-size tertile while the no-diet arm stays near zero in each, it holds on continuous pRF eccentricity and size within subject, and the estimator's own simulated bias is small, flat across ROIs and of the wrong sign (Extended Data Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables S7 and S8; Supplementary Note S3).

**The gradient does not stop at hV4, and it does not turn.** Relabelling the stored fits with NSD's full atlases (Extended Data Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S10), the diet index runs from −0.05 in V1v through −0.81 in hV4 and then sits between −0.81 and −0.94 in every extrastriate region — ventral, lateral and dorsal alike, in the Kastner2015 atlas, NSD's stream parcels and the fLoc category regions — with every region-minus-V1 contrast negative in 8/8 subjects (p = .0078) and no region-minus-region contrast departing from zero under either arm (all p > .05). Beyond V3 the diet's magno band contributes about 0.01 unique variance while the parvo band's rises from 0.10 in V1v to 0.26 in PHC2 (Extended Data Fig. 4b): the texture-biased stream carries extrastriate cortex as a whole, and the thesis's magno→dorsal, parvo→ventral mapping fails for want of any magno variance to map.

Three things this section does not settle. The diet predicts BOLD worse, not better (median r² normalised by the noise ceiling 0.405 against 0.464 without the diet, in 8/8 subjects; Fig. 3e), so its case rests on stream differentiation and the gradient, not on prediction accuracy. The two front-end controls are degenerate on this instrument (swap: U_p = 0.006; `parvo_highsf`: U_m = 0.007; degenerate in every one of their three seeds, Methods 4.5), so only the diet on luminance yields two streams that both carry substantial unique cortical variance and a gradient between them (Fig. 3a). And the gradient was not pre-specified: it is the survivor of seven regional hypotheses, six of which failed — among them the thesis's magno→dorsal mapping, an hMT+ specificity and a foveal/peripheral contrast whose sign flipped when the blur was removed — reported because it alone survives removal of that manipulation, and read as sign and ordering rather than as a calibrated effect (Supplementary Notes S1 and S3; Extended Data Table 1 for the subject-01 influence check).

**Results 2.4.**

The two streams are oppositely biased and the texture-biased one carries extrastriate cortex; what remains is what the behavioural readout does with them. The integrated readout's cue-conflict shape bias is 0.675 ± 0.025 with the diet against 0.416 ± 0.020 without (mean ± s.d. over three seeds, final checkpoints, as in Table 1; the thesis's checkpoint reproduces at 0.697 against its reported 0.677). On the identical images and statistic the ten human observers give 0.958 ± 0.019 (range 0.919–0.987) and the Geirhos et al. (2018a) CNNs 0.429, 0.312 and 0.172, values our pipeline reproduces. The diet-trained magno stream, at 0.878, closes most of the distance from the CNN regime toward the observers without matching the lowest of them, and integration costs 0.203 of shape bias at this checkpoint policy (0.878 → 0.675; 0.248 at the best saved checkpoints; Fig. 4c, Supplementary Tables S4 and S9).

The integrated readout lands between its two components — an outcome two oppositely biased components nearly force, so not a prediction — and what can be measured is how much of the rate the readout controls. Sweeping w while holding y₁ and y₂ exactly fixed (Fig. 4a) runs cue-conflict shape bias from 0.40 to 0.90 over 21 values of w, three seeds and both checkpoint policies; w = 0.5 reproduces each trained model exactly, and the two streams alone sit at 0.41 and 0.78 on the forced two-way estimator, so the span is not an artefact of the decided-image denominator (Supplementary Table S9). Because the affine sweep is exact interpolation between two measured endpoints, under a rule fixed before the test we refitted a two-layer nonlinear head, and a linear head as a control for the refit itself, on the frozen features at each w (Fig. 4b): the nonlinear head's median span over the three best-checkpoint seeds is 0.466 against 0.508 for the frozen affine head (92% of the pre-registered baseline, 86% in the weakest seed, threshold 80%), the refitted-linear and nonlinear medians (0.461 and 0.466) agree within 0.01, and restricted to readouts within 5 points of peak validation accuracy the nonlinear span is 0.283 (0.26–0.31 across seeds): the span is a property of two oppositely biased components under any readout, and the extreme rates near w = 1 are bought with accuracy.

On the ladder of Fig. 4c the two streams alone bracket the integrated readout (0.903 and 0.400 around 0.655) and the single-stream controls trained with the identical diet lie between (0.567 and 0.625), inside the 0.058 that capacity alone moves the rate, so the ordering among these three models is attributed to capacity and no shape-bias advantage is claimed for the split.

Nothing here fits w to humans, and it could not be: fitted to each of the ten observers individually on a 0.001 grid, no observer's rate is reachable in any run (0 of 90 observer-runs, the closest approach missing the lowest observer by 0.027), and a weight fitted to an observer's trial-by-trial choices does not cross-validate (held-out agreement +0.026 against +0.023 for an arbitrary weight, better in 45 of 90). The rate is a free parameter of the readout, and nothing in this family of readouts selects the human operating point.

**The readout weight cortex prefers descends the hierarchy.** The readout's input z(w) = w·y₁ + (1 − w)·y₂ is a feature vector like any other, so for each w it was the design of a ridge encoding model of every voxel in NSD's general visual mask, and the w that maximises an ROI's median r² is the weight that region prefers (Methods 4.5; Extended Data Fig. 5; Extended Data Table 2). Under the diet that weight descends the hierarchy — 0.58 in V1 to 0.33–0.35 in every extrastriate group, ventral, lateral and dorsal alike, below V1 in 8/8 subjects (Δ = −0.252 [−0.283, −0.219], exact p = .0078) — and at the weight extrastriate cortex prefers the readout's rate is 0.56, below the trained 0.675 and far from the observers' 0.958. The weight that best reproduces the observers' trial-by-trial choices is not identified by these data: its median over observers is 0.67 (range 0.49–0.89), but each observer-run's bootstrap interval averages 0.69 wide on [0, 1] and covers the extrastriate optimum in 23 of 30 fits, only 2 of 30 fits beat a label-permutation null, and the fitted weight does not cross-validate (above), so we do not claim that cortex and behaviour prefer different weights (Extended Data Table 2). Without the diet the cortical gradient reverses (0.65 in V1 to 0.75–1.00 beyond hV4; Δ = +0.188 [+0.077, +0.285], 7/8). The curves are shallow (every ROI within 3% of its best r² at w = 0.5 with the diet and within 6% without) and their heights sit at the ridge's own reproducibility — the 1,024-dimensional concatenation, which every mixture is a linear map of, is itself exceeded by the best mixture in 36 of 112 subject × ROI × arm cells by a median 0.0019 r², more than the 0.0016 the sweep buys — so this is an ordering of optima and never an r² difference: the dissociation of Fig. 3 in the readout's own currency.

**Without a stream split, a gain on a network's own shape- and texture-predictive subspaces still moves the rate.** The span of Fig. 4a is given by construction in a network built around two streams, so we tested networks with none, under plans committed before each analysis was run (Methods 4.8). In any frozen network the penultimate features hold a shape-predictive and a texture-predictive subspace — the fifteen discriminant directions of a 16-class shape and of a 16-class texture linear discriminant, made mutually orthogonal — and a gain of 2w on the first and 2(1 − w) on the second, everything else including the network's own head untouched, is the analogue of the mixing weight; every w is gated on top-1 accuracy on held-out natural images (Fig. 6). Because the 1,200 cue-conflict images reuse 160 object and 48 texture exemplars, the subspaces are fitted on images sharing no exemplar with those the rate is scored on (Methods 4.8); the pre-registered split shared exemplars, and its values are given as exemplar-shared. In the six ImageNet networks of Fig. 6 the rate rises monotonically with w (ρ ≥ 0.999 on the mean curve) by 0.14–0.20 with top-1 within 1.5 points of the trained value over the whole sweep (3.5 points on the held-out bases) — about a third of the two-stream span — so the pre-stated threshold (≥ 0.20 in at least four of the six), met in all six with exemplars shared (0.25–0.33), is met in one; four of the six can still be moved past the trained rates of at least two others, but the reverse of their trained order, reachable with exemplars shared, no longer is (Fig. 6a, b; Extended Data Tables 3 and 4). Across the field's zoo the result holds and the ranking does not: in 38 torchvision ImageNet classifiers the whole sweep stays within the five-point gate (top-1 changes by at most 4.4 points on the all-image bases; on the held-out bases the gate narrows in three networks, Methods 4.8), the reachable span is 0.08–0.29 (median 0.19; 0.20–0.50 with exemplars shared, the difference coming mostly through the texture exemplars; Methods 4.8) and unrelated to the trained rate (ρ = +0.10), the rankings by trained rate and by the rate at the shape end of the gate agree at Kendall τ = 0.63 with 19% of the 703 pairs reversing (16% on the forced estimator; 27% and 21% with exemplars shared), against 8.9% [6.0%, 12.2%] for the trained ranking re-estimated on resampled images, and the ranking by trained rate agrees with that by a linear probe's shape-minus-texture accuracy on held-out exemplars only at τ = 0.43 (Extended Data Fig. 6; Extended Data Table 4). The span needs the labelled directions: a gain on pairs of random 15-dimensional subspaces of the same features moves no network's rate by more than 0.08 (median 0.01 over ten draws each; Supplementary Table S14), below every network's span on unseen exemplars. Error consistency with the ten observers (Results 2.5), added after the pre-registered analysis, is 2–23% of the human–human ceiling at the trained readouts (median 8%); it rises with the trained rate across networks (ρ = 0.80) and, within every network, from the texture end to the shape end of its gate (38 of 38; median +14 points of the ceiling), yet no gated readout of an ImageNet-trained network exceeds 37% of the ceiling — a rise that is mostly the measure's own bound moving with the rate (Results 2.5; Supplementary Table S13). The informative exception is a ResNet-50 trained on Stylized-ImageNet (Geirhos et al., 2018a): the highest trained rate of the 42 networks tested (0.81), the only one whose probe leans to shape on exemplars it was not trained on (+0.23 [+0.13, +0.34], against −0.52 to +0.01 for the ImageNet-trained networks, 37 of 38 below zero; −0.11 against −0.30 to −0.66 with exemplars shared), and the highest agreement with the observers (40% of the ceiling at its trained readout, 53% at its shape end) — its bias sits in the representation, which the rate alone could not have shown. Its span is among the smallest (0.12 on unseen exemplars, the 3rd smallest of 39; 0.17 and the smallest with exemplars shared, short of its pre-registered 0.20 threshold, P-Z1–P-Z4, the other three predictions met), but that ranking belongs to the estimator — in sample it is the 9th smallest of 39, and on the forced estimator the 16th — and what sets the network apart is which evidence the gain acts on (below). Our own single trunks move by 0.23–0.34 ungated (0.17–0.28 on unseen exemplars) but lose 7–15 points of MiniEcoset top-1 at the ends of the sweep (seed means; up to 12–17 points in single seeds, Extended Data Table 3), 0.14–0.22 within the gate (0.10–0.15 on unseen exemplars, Fig. 6a), and a head refitted at each w on the lossy sum of the two subspaces, the residual passed unchanged, keeps validation accuracy within 1.2 points of its ceiling in every seed (0.5–0.8 points as family means) while the rate still moves by 0.10–0.15 (two-layer head; 0.08–0.13 linear). The information the gain uses is distributed — the two subspaces hold 4–15% (shape) and 20–33% (texture) of the feature variance — and the pre-specified unit-level rule, a median split of the units by their shape-minus-texture η², reaches gated spans of only 0.07–0.16 (Supplementary Table S11); on the two-stream network the data-driven subspaces are not the streams (63–70% of the shape basis's energy on stream 1, against the pre-stated 90%) and the subspace gain moves the rate by 0.32 (0.25 on unseen exemplars) where the streams themselves give 0.46 and the readout weight of Fig. 4a 0.50, so the architectural split remains that network's most effective factorisation.

**What the gain acts on.** Post hoc, under a fifth plan committed before the analysis ran (`docs/PLAN_SPAN_2026-09-18.md`), we decomposed each network's decisions at its trained readout. Because the gain adds (2w − 1)·W(P_S − P_T)y to the logits, its first-order effect on each image's shape-minus-texture margin is a shape term, the evidence the head receives through the shape subspace, plus a texture term, the texture evidence it receives through the texture subspace, the first added and the second removed as w rises (Methods 4.8). This account reproduces every network's forced-choice curve (median largest error 0.009) and, reduced to two numbers per network, ranks the forced-choice spans at ρ = 0.95, but it ranks the reported, held-out spans only at ρ = 0.59, and of seven predictions two were met, four failed and one held in half (Supplementary Table S14). What it does show is which evidence the gain moves. In 37 of the 38 ImageNet networks the texture term is the larger (median 89% of the effect; ViT-B/32 is the exception, at 45%): the gain moves an ImageNet network toward shape by removing texture evidence more than by adding shape evidence. The texture share tracks the probe's representational bias about as closely as it tracks the trained rate when the probe is scored on held-out exemplars (ρ = −0.58 [−0.78, −0.29] against +0.59), and more closely only with exemplars shared (−0.81 [−0.91, −0.62]); the Stylized-ImageNet network is its extreme at 11%, its texture term below every ImageNet network's (0.31 against 0.93–3.95 margin units, as predicted), the share returning to 75% and 91% as ImageNet is added to its training and then fine-tuned on.

**Cortex loads on the texture-leaning units, not on the discriminant subspaces.** With the two unit halves of the no-diet single trunk as encoding bands, the texture-leaning half carries every extrastriate group relative to V1 in 8/8 subjects (Δ = −0.32, exact p = .0078), and the cortex-optimal weight of the lossy sum falls from 0.80 in V1 to 0.37–0.48 beyond hV4 (7/8, p = .023) while the behavioural rate rises toward w = 1 (Fig. 6c, d; Supplementary Table S12); the diet-trained single ResNet-34 shows the same contrast (7/8, p = .016). In the ImageNet networks the texture-leaning half carries early visual cortex as well — V1 through hV4 and ventral cortex in ResNet-50, whose units are almost all texture-leaning, and every region in ConvNeXt-B (index −0.09 to −0.45) and ViT-B/16 (−0.13 to −0.31), two networks fitted after the pre-registered analysis — so the V1-relative prediction fails there (extrastriate − V1 = +0.24, p = .039; +0.22, 2/8; −0.08, 6/8, p = .25). These unique variances are small (≤ 0.04 for the texture-leaning half against 0.10–0.25 for the parvo band with the split, 80–96% of the explained variance being shared between the halves against 45–63% between the streams; ViT-B/16 is the least redundant, at up to 0.09 and 64–72%), and under the subspace rule they all but vanish: entered as three bands with the residual, the two 15-dimensional subspaces each add at most 1.3% of noise-ceiling-normalised unique variance in any region against 23–63% carried by the shared and residual variance, their index sits at noise level, and the pre-registered cortical predictions fail — the extrastriate − V1 contrast is negative in 2/8 subjects each in the no-diet trunk, the diet ResNet-34 and ResNet-50, in 7/8 in ConvNeXt-B (Δ = −0.44, p = .016) and in 3/8 in ViT-B/16, and the 15-dimensional lossy sum's cortex-optimal weight is lower beyond V1 in no family at the pre-registered 7/8. The directions that move the rate are nearly silent as unique cortical predictors — which component cortex loads on is decided by how the representation is partitioned — so the cortical claim is made under the unit rule only. The architecture is therefore not what makes the rate a readout property, nor, in the networks trained on our images, what makes extrastriate cortex load on the texture-biased component; it is what makes the components separable enough for one weight to move the rate across the whole range models occupy at no cost in accuracy, and for cortex to be carried almost entirely by one of them.

**Results 2.5.**

If shape bias is a property of a readout over components rather than of a representation, quantities the field treats as travelling with it need not. Over identical inputs, three do not (Fig. 5).

**Robustness.** The standard reading of Geirhos et al. (2018a, 2021) is that shape bias buys out-of-distribution robustness. We pre-registered that prediction and tested it on corruptions applied before the front end, scored in the models' own label space at each seed's best saved checkpoint (Fig. 5a; Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S5). The diet-trained model is less robust on four of the five corruption families it was not trained on — uniform noise, high-pass, false colour and rotation (relative-accuracy AUC 0.634 against 0.729, 0.678 against 0.827, 0.854 against 0.938 and 0.574 against 0.615), with complete seed separation on each — equivalent within ±0.033 on phase scrambling, more robust only to low-pass (0.444 against 0.356), declared trained-in before the run because the diet is a low-pass blur, and not to contrast reduction (0.481 against 0.549) although its front end includes a contrast-sensitivity cut. Robustness appears exactly where it was trained in and nowhere else, the strongest available evidence that the nulls are real rather than a broken measurement. This does not contradict Geirhos et al., whose stylisation route yields shape bias together with robustness — it shows the coupling belongs to that route — and it differs from the source of the diet, whose title reports robust, shape-based vision: we reproduce the shape-based half (their released model 0.822, our diet-trained magno stream 0.878) and not the robust half, and Methods 4.6 lists three implementation differences any of which could carry the divergence.

**Agreement with human decisions.** Two decision-makers with the same marginal rate can agree on no individual image beyond what those rates force; error consistency — Cohen's κ on trial-by-trial agreement — subtracts that forced agreement (Geirhos et al., 2020), and across the ten observers the human–human ceiling is κ = 0.234. We pre-registered that the diet-trained model would be more human-consistent than its control; it is not, on either readout, and the sharpest true statement is stronger: our most shape-biased model, the diet-trained magno stream at 0.878, has, averaged over seeds, the lowest error consistency of the seven models compared (κ = 0.0082, 3.5% of the ceiling, seeds spanning −6.1% to 15.5%, against 7.3% for VGG-16 at shape bias 0.172; Supplementary Table S6); across all 189 readouts of the w sweep, with w chosen post hoc per run to maximise agreement, the best κ never exceeds 20.7% of the ceiling (Fig. 5b), and equivalence intervals fixed in advance exclude a κ change larger than 0.038 across the 0.71 span of shape bias the readouts cover; across the field's zoo the same measure does rise with the rate, to at most 37% of the ceiling (Results 2.4) — and that rise is the measure's own bound. Cohen's κ cannot exceed a value fixed by the two marginals, which with the observers at 0.958 runs from 0.006 to 0.219 across the 798 gated readouts of the 38 networks; one quadratic in the rate accounts for the agreement (R² = 0.90) and, normalised by the bound, its rank dependence on the rate disappears (0.001 against 0.891). Agreement is thus readout-controlled as the rate is: over the 33 networks that reach a common rate of 0.40, ranking by agreement at the trained readouts against at that rate gives τ = 0.33 with 34% of pairs reversing, the 1.9-fold residual spread is unrelated to the probe (ρ = +0.01), and the Stylized-ImageNet network is not separable from ViT-B/32 (135% against 120% of the human-pair value). Four of six predictions of a fourth plan failed in this way and a fifth was untestable as specified (`docs/PLAN_KAPPA_2026-09-17.md`, post hoc here; Supplementary Table S13). The measure does detect a correspondence when one is there — the same machinery recovers 24.1% of the split-half ceiling on whether an image resolves to either cue at all, against 8.7% on which cue is picked (Fig. 5c) — a positive control that invites a diet reading we do not make, because the crossed front-end 2×2 shows a train/test-matching pattern rather than a weights effect (Fig. 5d).

**Representational dimensionality.** The participation ratio of each stream's features on 1,000 NSD images per subject gives a magno/parvo ratio whose ordering across the four front ends is reproduced by a learning-free Gabor bank, so it tracks the bandwidth of the carrier a stream is handed; what the diet decides is which side of parity training ends on — every diet-trained seed ends magno-dominant (24/24 subject-seeds in each of three diet arms), including the `parvo_highsf` front end, which starts inverted in 8/8 subjects and which we had pre-registered would stay inverted, while every no-diet seed collapses to parity (Extended Data Fig. 2). No level is claimed, because the within-arm seed range exceeds the gaps between arms; and `parvo_highsf`'s second stream becomes the more dimensional stream while remaining texture-leaning (probe −0.139 on held-out exemplars, −0.271 shared), which separates dimensionality from shape bias.

**Discussion.**

**The two questions.** The thesis asked whether magnocellular- and parvocellular-driven streams inside one network could diverge while the network behaves shape-biased, and whether such a system bears on why the ventral stream can be texture-like while behaviour is not. To the first the answer is yes, replicated across seeds and with the mechanism located: divergence requires the developmental transform on an achromatic carrier, the shape-biased component starts in the input and is kept, and extended, by training only under the diet and beside a second stream, and the texture-biased component is built by learning. To the second the answer is an existence proof the thesis could not reach with its instruments: fitted to human cortex, the texture-biased stream's unique variance carries the extrastriate end of the hierarchy — ventral, lateral and dorsal alike — while behaviour, reading out from both, is shape-biased. Read as competing claims about a single representation the two cannot both be right; read as claims about a readout over components, they are not in competition.

**What the readout account licenses.** A model matching the human shape-bias rate is routinely described as having human-like representations; that inference fails twice over here. The same network holds components at +0.34 and −0.17 on held-out exemplars (+0.82 and −0.52 with exemplars shared) and behaves at 0.675, and one scalar over frozen representations moves the rate across most of the range the field's models occupy, so the rate does not identify a representation; and across readouts spanning a five-fold range of shape bias, trial-by-trial agreement with ten observers never exceeds 21% of the human–human ceiling in our networks and is lowest, 3.5%, for our most shape-biased stream; across the zoo the two do move together (ρ = 0.80 over 38 networks, every network's agreement rising from the texture to the shape end of its gate), yet no gated readout exceeds 37% of the ceiling and that rise is the bound on κ moving with the rate (Supplementary Table S13), so the rate is at best a weak proxy for the decision process — as is agreement, read as the field reads it. Prior work established that a representation can hold more shape than its behaviour reveals (Hermann et al., 2020; Islam et al., 2021), that the measured rate depends on the test design (Tartaglini et al., 2022), that a prompt — a readout-level manipulation — steers it in vision–language models (Gavrikov et al., 2024), and that it tracks a network's spatial-frequency channel (Subramanian et al., 2023), the quantity the diet constrains; new here is the form of the dissociation — both representations held fixed, the rate moved by the readout alone, a refitted nonlinear head preserving the span — and its cortical grounding: the same mixing weight, fitted to cortex, prefers the magno-heavy end in V1 and the parvo-heavy end in every region beyond it (Extended Data Fig. 5); where human choices place it is not identified by these data. Nor does the account need the split: in 38 frozen ImageNet networks with none, a reciprocal gain on the network's own shape- and texture-predictive subspaces moves the rate by 0.08–0.29 on exemplars those subspaces never saw, within five points of top-1, re-ordering 19% of network pairs, twice the resampling noise, and in our single trunks the texture-leaning units again carry extrastriate cortex relative to V1 (Fig. 6); the split amplifies the dissociation (0.50 against 0.32 on the same network, 0.25 on unseen exemplars), it does not create it.

**What a developmental constraint does.** The intuitive account of low-pass developmental input is informational — coarse input shields the learner from texture, so it learns shape — and our results do not support it: the transform removes bandwidth rather than adding structure, most of the shape bias it produces is present before training, and what the diet decides is whether learning builds on that bias or destroys it (on held-out exemplars, training drives the luminance stream from +0.09 to −0.12 without it and from +0.21 to +0.34 with it, and a second instrument replicates the side of parity rather than a level). This is a constraint on the solution learning converges to, not a better input representation, consistent with the thesis's observation that the transformed magno stream becomes less predictive of the category as the parvo stream becomes more so.

**What comes apart, and what the diet costs.** Shape bias, robustness, dimensionality and human-likeness are discussed as a package; over identical inputs this system separates them three times, and a readout account says why it should: none of the three is a property of a readout. The diet is also expensive — less accurate (0.556 against 0.701), less stable late in training, less robust on four of five untrained-on corruptions, a worse predictor of BOLD — so nothing here recommends it as a training method; its interest is as a model of development, and the thesis's central mechanistic reading, that divergence and shape bias are bought by degrading the magno stream's evidence, survives in every instrument.

**How to report shape bias.** A shape-bias claim should carry three numbers, each answering a different question: the rate at the trained readout (an operating point); the span the same frozen features reach under a reciprocal gain on their own shape- and texture-predictive subspaces within an accuracy budget (how much of the rate the readout controls); and a probe's shape-minus-texture accuracy (what the representation holds) — the last two scored on exemplars the subspaces and probe were not fitted on, since the standard cue-conflict set reuses 48 texture images across 1,200 stimuli and exemplar-shared estimates inflate both. Across 38 ImageNet networks the three rank the zoo differently (τ = 0.63 and 0.43), and the Stylized-ImageNet network shows what their agreement looks like when a bias really is representational — seen from the readout, the difference is not how far the gain moves it but which evidence the gain moves (Results 2.4); error consistency does not make a fourth: read at each network's own readout it is largely a re-expression of the rate, because κ is bounded by the mismatch with the observers' 0.958, and at a matched rate it separates neither the zoo nor the Stylized-ImageNet network from it, so where human trial data exist it should be read at a matched rate (Supplementary Table S13). None of these numbers alone licenses a claim about representations. One command in the repository prints all three, with the exemplar-shared values beside them, for any torchvision network (Methods 4.8).

**Limitations (sentences condensed).** Beyond hV4 the index saturates near its bound in every atlas, so the extension establishes that the effect is hierarchy-wide but cannot order extrastriate regions against one another. The `parvo_highsf` condition varies architecture alongside the front end. The diet is a fixed 48-month front end rather than a trajectory, though a schedule-trained checkpoint behaves like it on every instrument we tried. The single-trunk subspaces are linear discriminants of one cue-conflict set, and their spans are lower bounds on what a readout can reach, not measurements of the best one.

## Supplementary Note S5 — the pre-registration ledgers

The generality test was pre-registered three times and all three ledgers are complete (Methods 4.8; outcomes in the plan files): of the unit-level plan's five predictions, the behavioural thresholds were met by direction but not by magnitude, the stream-recovery check was met, the V1-relative cortical contrast held in our two trunks and reversed in ImageNet ResNet-50, and the mixing-weight ordering held in one network of three; of the subspace plan's, the magnitude threshold was met in all six ImageNet networks but in one of our three trunks, the stream-recovery check failed (63–70% of the shape basis on stream 1, against 90%), the subspace span exceeded the unit-level span in every network (Extended Data Tables 3 and 4; Supplementary Tables S11 and S12), and of its three cortical predictions only the ConvNeXt-B contrast held, the two discriminant subspaces carrying almost no unique cortical variance; of the zoo plan's four predictions three were met and the Stylized-ImageNet span fell short of its threshold, read, as pre-stated, as a bias held in the representation rather than in the readout — a reading of the span that a fifth plan does not support (Results 2.4). A fourth plan, committed before its own analysis ran, predicted that agreement with the observers would separate representations at a matched rate; four of its six predictions failed, for the reason given in Results 2.5. A fifth, on what sets the span, met two of its seven predictions; which evidence the gain moves is a post hoc reading of it (Results 2.4). A sixth, re-estimating spans and probes with exemplars held out, is post hoc with respect to the five before it, written after a referee simulation found the exemplar reuse; it met 15 of 17 predictions; the two failures concern the probe, whose ImageNet networks stay texture-leaning where we predicted they would not (`docs/PLAN_EXEMPLAR_2026-09-21.md`). **Supplementary Table S14 | What the gain acts on: the span decomposed.** Every frozen network of Extended Data Table 4, at its trained readout. For each cue-conflict image the Geirhos rule gives a margin Δ = log p_shape − log p_texture (p_c the mean softmax probability over category c's ImageNet classes; the forced two-way estimator calls the image “shape” iff Δ > 0). The gain of Methods 4.8 adds (2w − 1)·W(P_S − P_T)y to the logits exactly, so to first order it changes Δ by (2w − 1)(e_S − e_T), with e_S and e_T the evidence the head receives through the shape and the texture subspace (u·W P_S y and u·W P_T y, u the gradient of Δ with respect to the logits). “Shape term” is the mean of e_S over the 1,200 images (evidence added at the shape end), “texture term” the mean of −e_T (texture evidence removed there), both in units of the margin; texture share = texture term / (shape term + texture term). M0 is the first-order forced-choice span from the per-image margins, M1 the two-number span Φ(z + λ) − Φ(z − λ) with z = mean Δ / sd Δ and λ = mean(e_S − e_T) / sd Δ. “Reported” is the held-out span of Extended Data Table 4 (primary estimator, on halves that share exemplars); “in sample” is the same estimator and “forced” the forced two-way estimator, both with the bases fitted on all 1,200 images. Random-direction span: ten draws (seeds 0–9) of two mutually orthogonal random 15-dimensional subspaces inside each network's 256-dimensional PCA space of the cue-conflict features, same gain, estimator (in sample) and gate; every random sweep stays inside its gate.
